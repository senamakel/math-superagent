[1] The Newton polytope of the [short Weierstrass equation][2] y 2 = x 3 + a x + b {\displaystyle y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b}[image: {\displaystyle y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b}]. The green points correspond to the powers of the polynomials in the equations. Since there is one integer interior lattice point, then the [genus][3] of the equation also equals to one. [1]

In mathematics, the **Newton polytope**is an [integral polytope][4] associated with a multivariate [polynomial][5] that can be used in the [asymptotic analysis][6] of those polynomials. It is a generalization of the Kruskal – Newton diagram developed for the analysis of bivariant polynomials.

Given a vector x = ( x 1, …, x n) {\displaystyle \mathbf {x} =(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})}[image: {\displaystyle \mathbf {x} =(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})}] of variables and a finite family ( a k) k {\displaystyle (\mathbf {a} _{k})_{k}}[image: {\displaystyle (\mathbf {a} _{k})_{k}}] of pairwise distinct vectors from N n {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} ^{n}}[image: {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} ^{n}}] each encoding the exponents within a monomial, consider the multivariate polynomial

f ( x) = ∑ k c k x a k {\displaystyle f(\mathbf {x} )=\sum _{k}c_{k}\mathbf {x} ^{\mathbf {a} _{k}}}[image: {\displaystyle f(\mathbf {x} )=\sum _{k}c_{k}\mathbf {x} ^{\mathbf {a} _{k}}}]

where we use the shorthand notation ( x 1, …, x n) ( y 1, …, y n) {\displaystyle (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})^{(y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n})}}[image: {\displaystyle (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})^{(y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n})}}] for the monomial x 1 y 1 x 2 y 2 ⋯ x n y n {\displaystyle x_{1}^{y_{1}}x_{2}^{y_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{y_{n}}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{1}^{y_{1}}x_{2}^{y_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{y_{n}}}]. Then the Newton polytope associated to f {\displaystyle f}[image: {\displaystyle f}] is the [convex hull][7] of the vectors a k {\displaystyle \mathbf {a} _{k}}[image: {\displaystyle \mathbf {a} _{k}}]; that is

Newt ⁡ ( f) = { ∑ k α k a k: ∑ k α k = 1 & ∀ j α j ≥ 0 }. {\displaystyle \operatorname {Newt} (f)=\left\{\sum _{k}\alpha _{k}\mathbf {a} _{k}:\sum _{k}\alpha _{k}=1\;\&\;\forall j\,\,\alpha _{j}\geq 0\right\}\!.}[image: {\displaystyle \operatorname {Newt} (f)=\left\{\sum _{k}\alpha _{k}\mathbf {a} _{k}:\sum _{k}\alpha _{k}=1\;\&\;\forall j\,\,\alpha _{j}\geq 0\right\}\!.}]

In order to make this well-defined, we assume that all coefficients c k {\displaystyle c_{k}}[image: {\displaystyle c_{k}}] are non-zero. The Newton polytope satisfies the following homomorphism-type property: Newt ⁡ ( f g) = Newt ⁡ ( f) + Newt ⁡ ( g) {\displaystyle \operatorname {Newt} (fg)=\operatorname {Newt} (f)+\operatorname {Newt} (g)}[image: {\displaystyle \operatorname {Newt} (fg)=\operatorname {Newt} (f)+\operatorname {Newt} (g)}] where the addition is in [the sense of Minkowski][8].

Newton polytopes are the central object of study in [tropical geometry][9] and characterize the [Gröbner bases for an ideal][10].
