The **magic square of squares**is an [unsolved problem in mathematics][2] which asks whether it is possible to construct a three-by-three [magic square][3], the elements of which are all [square numbers][4]. The problem was first posed by Martin LaBar in the January 1984 College Mathematics Journal, before being included in [Richard Guy][5] 's *Unsolved problems in number theory (2nd edition)*in 1994. [1] [2]

The problem has been a popular choice for [recreational mathematicians][6] following two articles [Martin Gardner][7] published in *[Quantum Magazine][8]*on the problem, offering a prize of [US$][9] 100 in 1996. [3] [4] Other prizes have subsequently been offered for the first solution. [5]

## Background

[[edit source][10]]

Main article: [Magic square][3]

[11] The smallest (and unique [up to][12] rotation and reflection) non-trivial case of a magic square, order 3

A [magic square][3] is a square array of [integer][13] numbers in which each row, column, and diagonal sums to the same number. [6] The order of the square refers to the number of integers along each side. [7] A **[trivial][14] magic square**is a magic square which has at least one repeated element, and a **semimagic square**is a magic square in which the rows and columns, but not both diagonals, sum to the same number.

## Problem

[[edit source][15]]

The problem asks whether it is possible to construct a third-order magic square such that every element is itself a square number. A square which solves the problem would thus be of the form

x 1 2 {\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}}] | x 2 2 {\displaystyle x_{2}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{2}^{2}}] | x 3 2 {\displaystyle x_{3}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{3}^{2}}] |

x 4 2 {\displaystyle x_{4}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{4}^{2}}] | x 5 2 {\displaystyle x_{5}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{5}^{2}}] | x 6 2 {\displaystyle x_{6}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{6}^{2}}] |

x 7 2 {\displaystyle x_{7}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{7}^{2}}] | x 8 2 {\displaystyle x_{8}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{8}^{2}}] | x 9 2 {\displaystyle x_{9}^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{9}^{2}}] |

and satisfy the following equations [8]

x 1 2 + x 2 2 + x 3 2 = x 4 2 + x 5 2 + x 6 2 = x 7 2 + x 8 2 + x 9 2 = x 1 2 + x 5 2 + x 9 2 = x 3 2 + x 5 2 + x 7 2 = x 1 2 + x 4 2 + x 7 2 = x 2 2 + x 5 2 + x 8 2 = x 3 2 + x 6 2 + x 9 2 {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}&=x_{4}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{6}^{2}\\&=x_{7}^{2}+x_{8}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\\&=x_{1}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\\&=x_{3}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{7}^{2}\\&=x_{1}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}+x_{7}^{2}\\&=x_{2}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{8}^{2}\\&=x_{3}^{2}+x_{6}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\end{aligned}}}[image: {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}&=x_{4}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{6}^{2}\\&=x_{7}^{2}+x_{8}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\\&=x_{1}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\\&=x_{3}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{7}^{2}\\&=x_{1}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}+x_{7}^{2}\\&=x_{2}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}+x_{8}^{2}\\&=x_{3}^{2}+x_{6}^{2}+x_{9}^{2}\end{aligned}}}]

## Current research

[[edit source][16]]

It has been shown that the problem is equivalent to several other problems. [2]

1. Do there exist three [arithmetic progressions][17] such that each has three terms, each has the same difference between terms as the other two, the terms are all [perfect squares][4], and the middle terms of the three arithmetic progressions themselves form an arithmetic progression?
2. Do there exist three [rational][18] [right triangles][19] with the same [area][20], such that the squares of the [hypotenuses][21] are in arithmetic progression?
3. Does there exist an [elliptic curve][22], y 2 = x 3 − n 2 x {\displaystyle y^{2}=x^{3}-n^{2}x}[image: {\displaystyle y^{2}=x^{3}-n^{2}x}], where n {\displaystyle n}[image: {\displaystyle n}] is a [congruent number][23], with three rational points on the curve, ( x 1, y 1) {\displaystyle (x_{1},y_{1})}[image: {\displaystyle (x_{1},y_{1})}], ( x 2, y 2) {\displaystyle (x_{2},y_{2})}[image: {\displaystyle (x_{2},y_{2})}], ( x 3, y 3) {\displaystyle (x_{3},y_{3})}[image: {\displaystyle (x_{3},y_{3})}], such that each point is "double" another rational point on the curve ("double" in the sense of [the group structure][24] for points on an elliptic curve), and x 1 {\displaystyle x_{1}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{1}}], x 2 {\displaystyle x_{2}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{2}}] and x 3 {\displaystyle x_{3}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{3}}] are in arithmetic progression?

### Properties of a magic square of squares

[[edit source][25]]

Let S {\displaystyle S}[image: {\displaystyle S}] denote the sum of each row, column or main diagonals of a *primitive*(all cells are pairwise [coprime][26]) third-order magic square of squares. If such a magic square of squares exists, it must satisfy the following properties.

- All cells must be odd. [9]
- All cells must be of the form 24 k + 1 {\displaystyle 24k+1}[image: {\displaystyle 24k+1}]. [10]
- No cell can have a prime divisor p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the form p = 8 k + 3 {\displaystyle p=8k+3}[image: {\displaystyle p=8k+3}]. [9]
- S {\displaystyle S}[image: {\displaystyle S}] must be of the form S = 3 a 2 {\displaystyle S=3a^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle S=3a^{2}}] where a 2 {\displaystyle a^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle a^{2}}] is the middle cell. [11]
- S {\displaystyle S}[image: {\displaystyle S}] must be of the form S = 72 k + 3 {\displaystyle S=72k+3}[image: {\displaystyle S=72k+3}]. [10]
- All prime divisors p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the middle cell must be of the form p = 4 k + 1 {\displaystyle p=4k+1}[image: {\displaystyle p=4k+1}]. [9]
- A prime p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the form p = 8 k + 5 {\displaystyle p=8k+5}[image: {\displaystyle p=8k+5}] cannot divide a non-corner edge cell. [9]
- If a prime p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the form p = 8 k + 3 {\displaystyle p=8k+3}[image: {\displaystyle p=8k+3}] divides a non-center cell then p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] must also divide the center cell and the other cell in that line. [9]
- If a prime p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the form p = 4 k + 3 {\displaystyle p=4k+3}[image: {\displaystyle p=4k+3}] divides a corner cell then it must also divide the two non-corner edge cells that are not adjacent to that corner. [9]
- If a prime p {\displaystyle p}[image: {\displaystyle p}] of the form p = 8 k + 5 {\displaystyle p=8k+5}[image: {\displaystyle p=8k+5}] divides a corner cell then it must also divide the center cell and the opposite corner cell. [12]

[Brute force][27] searches for solutions have been unsuccessful, and suggest that if a solution exists, it would consist of numbers greater than at least 10 14 {\displaystyle 10^{14}}[image: {\displaystyle 10^{14}}]. [13]

[Rice University][28] professor of mathematics Anthony Várilly-Alvarado has expressed his doubt as to the existence of the magic square of squares. [8]

## Notable attempts

[[edit source][29]]

There have been a number of attempts to construct a magic square of squares by [recreational mathematicians][6].

### Sallows' Square

[[edit source][30]]

Following Gardner's prize offer for anyone who could find a magic square of squares in 1996, [Lee Sallows][31] published his attempt in *[The Mathematical Intelligencer][32]*. His attempt is unique in that all of the rows and columns, and one of the diagonals, all sum to the same square number. [14] [8]

Sallows' Square [14]

 | 127 2 | 46 2 | 58 2 | **147 2** |

 | 2 2 | 113 2 | 94 2 | **147 2** |

 | 74 2 | 82 2 | 97 2 | **147 2** |

**147 2** | **147 2** | **147 2** | **147 2** | **38307** |

### Bremner Square

[[edit source][33]]

In 1999, Andrew Bremner published his attempt at the problem, and further research surrounding magic squares of squares. [15] Bremner's attempt differs from others in that not all elements of the square are square numbers, while all the rows, columns and diagonals sum to the same number. [8]

Bremner Square [15]

 | 373 2 | 289 2 | 565 2 | **541875** |

 | 360721 | 425 2 | 23 2 | **541875** |

 | 205 2 | 527 2 | 222121 | **541875** |

**541875** | **541875** | **541875** | **541875** | **541875** |

### Parker square

[[edit source][34]]

The **Parker square**[16] is an attempt by [Matt Parker][35] to solve the problem. His solution is a trivial, semimagic square of squares, as 41 2 {\displaystyle 41^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle 41^{2}}], 29 2 {\displaystyle 29^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle 29^{2}}] and 1 2 {\displaystyle 1^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle 1^{2}}] all appear twice, and the diagonal 23 2 + 37 2 + 47 2 {\displaystyle 23^{2}+37^{2}+47^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle 23^{2}+37^{2}+47^{2}}] sums to 4107 instead of 3051. [17] [13]

 | 41 2 | 37 2 | 1 2 | **3051** |

 | 23 2 | 41 2 | 29 2 | **3051** |

**4107** | **3051** | **3051** | **3051** | **3051** |

## Non third-order magic squares of squares

[[edit source][36]]

Magic squares of squares of orders greater than 3 have been known since as early as 1770, when [Leonhard Euler][37] sent a letter to [Joseph-Louis Lagrange][38] detailing a fourth-order magic square. [18]

Euler's magic square of squares

68 2 | 29 2 | 41 2 | 37 2 |

17 2 | 31 2 | 79 2 | 32 2 |

59 2 | 28 2 | 23 2 | 61 2 |

11 2 | 77 2 | 8 2 | 49 2 |

**Multimagic squares**are magic squares which remain magic after raising every element to some [power][39]. In 1890, Georges Pfeffermann published a solution to a problem he posed involving the construction of an eighth-order 2-multimagic square. [19]
