Unsolved problem in mathematics

Are there infinitely many unitary perfect numbers?

[More unsolved problems in mathematics][1]

A **unitary perfect number**is an [integer][2] which is the sum of its positive [proper][3] [unitary divisors][4], not including the number itself. (A [divisor][5]*d*of a number *n*is a unitary divisor if *d*and *n*/*d*share no common [factors][5].) The number 6 is the only number that is both a [perfect number][6] and a unitary perfect number.

## Known examples

[[edit][7]]

The number [60][8] is a unitary perfect number because 1, 3, 4, 5, 12, 15, and 20 are its proper unitary divisors, and 1 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 12 + 15 + 20 = 60. The first five, and only known, unitary perfect numbers are:

- 6 = 2 × 3 {\displaystyle 6=2\times 3}[image: {\displaystyle 6=2\times 3}]
- 60 = 2 2 × 3 × 5 {\displaystyle 60=2^{2}\times 3\times 5}[image: {\displaystyle 60=2^{2}\times 3\times 5}]
- 90 = 2 × 3 2 × 5 {\displaystyle 90=2\times 3^{2}\times 5}[image: {\displaystyle 90=2\times 3^{2}\times 5}]
- 87360 = 2 6 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 13 {\displaystyle 87360=2^{6}\times 3\times 5\times 7\times 13}[image: {\displaystyle 87360=2^{6}\times 3\times 5\times 7\times 13}], and
- 146361946186458562560000 = 2 18 × 3 × 5 4 × 7 × 11 × 13 × 19 × 37 × 79 × 109 × 157 × 313 {\displaystyle 146361946186458562560000=2^{18}\times 3\times 5^{4}\times 7\times 11\times 13\times 19\times 37\times 79\times 109\times 157\times 313}[image: {\displaystyle 146361946186458562560000=2^{18}\times 3\times 5^{4}\times 7\times 11\times 13\times 19\times 37\times 79\times 109\times 157\times 313}] (sequence [A002827][9] in the [OEIS][10]).

The respective sums of their proper unitary divisors are as follows:

- [6][11] = 1 + 2 + 3
- 60 = 1 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 12 + 15 + 20
- [90][12] = 1 + 2 + 5 + 9 + 10 + 18 + 45
- 87360 = 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 13 + 15 + 21 + 35 + 39 + 64 + 65 + 91 + 105 + 192 + 195 + 273 + 320 + 448 + 455 + 832 + 960 + 1344 + 1365 + 2240 + 2496 + 4160 + 5824 + 6720 + 12480 + 17472 + 29120
- 146361946186458562560000 = 1 + 3 + 7 + 11 + ... + 13305631471496232960000 + 20908849455208366080000 + 48787315395486187520000 (4095 divisors in the sum)

## Properties

[[edit][13]]

There are no [odd][14] unitary perfect numbers. This follows since 2*d**(*n*) divides the sum of the unitary divisors of an odd number *n*, where *d**(*n*) is the number of distinct [prime][15] factors of *n*. One gets this because the sum of all the unitary divisors is a [multiplicative function][16] and one has that the sum of the unitary divisors of a [prime power][17]*p**a*is *p**a*+ 1 which is [even][14] for all odd primes *p*. Therefore, an odd unitary perfect number must have only one distinct prime factor, and it is not hard to show that a power of prime cannot be a unitary perfect number, since there are not enough divisors.

It is not known whether or not there are infinitely many unitary perfect numbers, or indeed whether there are any further examples beyond the five already known. A sixth such number would have at least nine distinct odd prime factors. [1]
