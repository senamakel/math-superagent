# Goldbach Conjecture

---

[image: DOWNLOAD Mathematica Notebook] [Download Wolfram Notebook][1]

Goldbach's original conjecture (sometimes called the "ternary" Goldbach conjecture), written in a June 7, 1742 letter to Euler, states "at least it seems that every number that is greater than 2 is the [sum][2] of three [primes][3] " (Goldbach 1742; Dickson 2005, p. 421). Note that Goldbach considered the number 1 to be a prime, a convention that is no longer followed. As re-expressed by Euler, an equivalent form of this [conjecture][4] (called the "strong" or "binary" Goldbach conjecture) asserts that all [positive][5] [even][6] [integers][7][image: >=4] can be expressed as the [sum][2] of two [primes][3]. Two primes [image: (p,q)] such that [image: p+q=2n] for [image: n] a positive integer are sometimes called a [Goldbach partition][8] (Oliveira e Silva).

According to Hardy (1999, p. 19), "It is comparatively easy to make clever guesses; indeed there are theorems, like 'Goldbach's Theorem,' which have never been proved and which any fool could have guessed." Faber and Faber offered a [image: $1000000] prize to anyone who proved Goldbach's conjecture between March 20, 2000 and March 20, 2002, but the prize went unclaimed and the conjecture remains open.

Schnirelman (1939) proved that every [even][6] number can be written as the sum of not more than [image: 300000] [primes][3] (Dunham 1990), which seems a rather far cry from a proof for *two*[primes][3]! Pogorzelski (1977) claimed to have proven the Goldbach conjecture, but his proof is not generally accepted (Shanks 1985). The conjecture that all [odd numbers][9][image: >=9] are the [sum][2] of three [odd primes][10] is called the "weak" Goldbach conjecture. Vinogradov (1937ab, 1954) proved that every [sufficiently large][11] [odd number][9] is the [sum][2] of three [primes][3] (Nagell 1951, p. 66; Guy 1994), and Estermann (1938) proved that almost all [even numbers][6] are the sums of two [primes][3]. Vinogradov's original "sufficiently large" [image: N>=3^(3^(15)) approx e^(e^(16.573)) approx 3.25×10^(6846168)] was subsequently reduced to [image: e^(e^(11.503)) approx 3.33×10^(43000)] by Chen and Wang (1989). Chen (1973, 1978) also showed that all sufficiently large [even numbers][6] are the sum of a [prime][3] and the [product][12] of at most two [primes][3] (Guy 1994, Courant and Robbins 1996). More than two and a half centuries after the original conjecture was stated, the weak Goldbach conjecture was proved by Helfgott (2013, 2014).

A stronger version of the weak conjecture, namely that every odd number [image: >=7] can be expressed as the sum of a prime plus twice a prime is known as [Levy's conjecture][13].

An equivalent statement of the Goldbach conjecture is that for every positive integer [image: m], there are [primes][3][image: p] and [image: q] such that

[image:  phi(p)+phi(q)=2m, ] |

where [image: phi(x)] is the [totient function][14] (e.g., Havil 2003, p. 115; Guy 2004, p. 160). (This follows immediately from [image: phi(p)=p-1] for [image: p] prime.) Erdős and Moser have considered dropping the restriction that [image: p] and [image: q] be prime in this equation as a possibly easier way of determining if such numbers always exist (Guy 1994, p. 105).

Other variants of the Goldbach conjecture include the statements that every [even number][6][image: >=6] is the [sum][2] of two [odd][9] [primes][3], and every [integer][7][image: >17] the sum of exactly three distinct [primes][3].

Let [image: R(n)] be the number of representations of an [even number][6][image: n] as the sum of two [primes][3]. Then the "extended" Goldbach conjecture states that

[image:  R(n)&sim;2Pi_2product_(k=2; p_k|n)(p_k-1)/(p_k-2)int_2^n(dx)/((lnx)^2), ] |

where [image: Pi_2] is the [twin primes constant][15] (Halberstam and Richert 1974).
