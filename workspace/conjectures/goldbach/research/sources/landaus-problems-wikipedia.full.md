Four basic unsolved problems about prime numbers

[1] [Edmund Landau][2], German mathematician

At the 1912 [International Congress of Mathematicians][3], [Edmund Landau][2] listed four basic problems about [prime numbers][4]. These problems were characterised in his speech as "unattackable at the present state of mathematics" and are now known as **Landau's problems**. They are as follows:

1. [Goldbach's conjecture][5]: Can every even [integer][6] greater than 2 be written as the sum of two primes?
2. [Twin prime conjecture][7]: Are there infinitely many primes *p*such that *p*+ 2 is prime?
3. [Legendre's conjecture][8]: Does there always exist at least one prime between consecutive [perfect squares][9]?
4. Are there infinitely many primes *p*such that *p*− 1 is a perfect square? In other words: Are there infinitely many primes of the form *n*2 + 1?

As of 2026 [[update]][10], all four problems are unresolved.

## Progress toward solutions

[[edit][11]]

### Goldbach's conjecture

[[edit][12]]

Main article: [Goldbach's conjecture][5]

[Goldbach's weak conjecture][13], every odd number greater than 5 can be expressed as the sum of three primes, is a consequence of [Goldbach's conjecture][5]. [Ivan Vinogradov][14] proved it for large enough *n*( [Vinogradov's theorem][15]) in 1937, [1] and [Harald Helfgott][16] extended this to a full proof of Goldbach's weak conjecture in 2013. [2] [3] [4]

[Chen's theorem][17], another weakening of Goldbach's conjecture, proves that for all sufficiently large *n*, 2 n = p + q {\displaystyle 2n=p+q}[image: {\displaystyle 2n=p+q}] where *p*is prime and *q*is either prime or [semiprime][18]. [note 1] Bordignon, Johnston, and Starichkova, [5] correcting and improving on Yamada, [6] proved an explicit version of Chen's theorem: every even number greater than e e 32.7 ≈ 1.4 ⋅ 10 69057979807814 {\displaystyle e^{e^{32.7}}\approx 1.4\cdot 10^{69057979807814}}[image: {\displaystyle e^{e^{32.7}}\approx 1.4\cdot 10^{69057979807814}}] is the sum of a prime and a product of at most two primes. Bordignon and Starichkova [7] reduce this to e e 15.85 ≈ 3.6 ⋅ 10 3321634 {\displaystyle e^{e^{15.85}}\approx 3.6\cdot 10^{3321634}}[image: {\displaystyle e^{e^{15.85}}\approx 3.6\cdot 10^{3321634}}] assuming the [Generalized Riemann hypothesis][19] (GRH) for [Dirichlet L-functions][20]. Johnston and Starichkova give a version working for all *n*≥ 4 at the cost of using a number which is the product of at most 395 primes rather than a prime or semiprime; under GRH they improve 395 to 31. [8]

[Montgomery][21] and [Vaughan][22] showed that the exceptional set of even numbers not expressible as the sum of two primes has a [density][23] zero, although the set is not proven to be finite. [9] The best current bounds on the exceptional set is E ( x) < x 0.72 {\displaystyle E(x)<x^{0.72}}[image: {\displaystyle E(x)<x^{0.72}}] (for large enough *x*) due to [Pintz][24], [10] [11] and E ( x) ≪ x 0.5 log 3 ⁡ x {\displaystyle E(x)\ll x^{0.5}\log ^{3}x}[image: {\displaystyle E(x)\ll x^{0.5}\log ^{3}x}] under [RH][25], due to [Goldston][26]. [12]

[Linnik][27] proved that large enough even numbers could be expressed as the sum of two primes and some ( [ineffective][28]) constant *K*of powers of 2. [13] Following many advances (see Pintz [14] for an overview), [Pintz][24] and [Ruzsa][29] [15] improved this to *K*= 8. Assuming the GRH, this can be improved to *K*= 7. [16]

### Twin prime conjecture

[[edit][30]]

Main article: [Twin prime conjecture][7]

In 2013 [Yitang Zhang][31] showed [17] that there are infinitely many prime pairs with gap bounded by 70 million, and this result has been improved to gaps of length 246 by a collaborative effort of the [Polymath Project][32]. [18] Under the generalized [Elliott–Halberstam conjecture][33] this was improved to 6, extending earlier work by [Maynard][34] [19] and [Goldston][26], [Pintz][24] and [Yıldırım][35]. [20]

In 1966 [Chen][36] showed that there are infinitely many primes *p*(later called [Chen primes][37]) such that *p*+ 2 is either a prime or a semiprime.

### Legendre's conjecture

[[edit][38]]

Main article: [Legendre's conjecture][8]

### Near-square primes

[[edit][42]]

Landau's fourth problem asked whether there are infinitely many primes which are of the form p = n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle p=n^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle p=n^{2}+1}] for integer *n*. (The list of known primes of this form is [A002496][43].) The existence of infinitely many such primes would follow as a consequence of other number-theoretic conjectures such as the [Bunyakovsky conjecture][44] and [Bateman–Horn conjecture][45].

One example of near-square primes are [Fermat primes][46]. [Henryk Iwaniec][47] showed that there are infinitely many numbers of the form n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}] with at most two prime factors. [26] [27] [Ankeny][48] [28] and [Kubilius][49] [29] proved that, assuming the [extended Riemann hypothesis][50] for **[L -functions][51] on [Hecke characters][52], there are infinitely many primes of the form p = x 2 + y 2 {\displaystyle p=x^{2}+y^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle p=x^{2}+y^{2}}] with y = O ( log ⁡ p) {\displaystyle y=O(\log p)}[image: {\displaystyle y=O(\log p)}]. Landau's conjecture is for the stronger y = 1 {\displaystyle y=1}[image: {\displaystyle y=1}]. The best unconditional result is due to Harman and Lewis [30] and it gives y = O ( p 0.119) {\displaystyle y=O(p^{0.119})}[image: {\displaystyle y=O(p^{0.119})}]. In 2024, [Ben Green][53] and Mehtaab Sawhney proved that infinitely many primes are of the form p 2 + n q 2 {\displaystyle p^{2}+nq^{2}}[image: {\displaystyle p^{2}+nq^{2}}] for any given n ≡ 0 {\displaystyle n\equiv 0}[image: {\displaystyle n\equiv 0}] or n ≡ 4 ( mod 6) {\displaystyle n\equiv 4}{\pmod {6}}[image: {\displaystyle n\equiv 4}{\pmod {6}}], noting that "the argument could surely be modified in a straightforward manner to handle arbitrary positive definite [binary quadratic forms][54] over Z {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} }[image: {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} }] ". [31]

Grimmelt & Merikoski, [32] improving on previous works, [33] [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] showed that there are infinitely many numbers of the form n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}] with greatest prime factor at least n 1.312 {\displaystyle n^{1.312}}[image: {\displaystyle n^{1.312}}]. Replacing the exponent with 2 would yield Landau's conjecture.

The [Friedlander–Iwaniec theorem][55] shows that infinitely many primes are of the form x 2 + y 4 {\displaystyle x^{2}+y^{4}}[image: {\displaystyle x^{2}+y^{4}}]. [39]

Baier and Zhao [40] prove that there are infinitely many primes of the form p = a n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle p=an^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle p=an^{2}+1}] with a < p 5 / 9 + ε {\displaystyle a<p^{5/9+\varepsilon }}[image: {\displaystyle a<p^{5/9+\varepsilon }}]; the exponent can be improved to 1 / 2 + ε {\displaystyle 1/2+\varepsilon }[image: {\displaystyle 1/2+\varepsilon }] under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for L-functions and to ε {\displaystyle \varepsilon }[image: {\displaystyle \varepsilon }] under a certain [Elliott-Halberstam][33] type hypothesis.

The [Brun sieve][56] establishes an upper bound on the density of primes having the form p = n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle p=n^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle p=n^{2}+1}]: there are O ( x / log ⁡ x) {\displaystyle O({\sqrt {x}}/\log x)}[image: {\displaystyle O({\sqrt {x}}/\log x)}] such primes up to x {\displaystyle x}[image: {\displaystyle x}]. Hence [almost all][57] numbers of the form n 2 + 1 {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}[image: {\displaystyle n^{2}+1}] are composite.
