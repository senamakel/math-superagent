**Goldbach's conjecture**is one of the oldest and best-known [unsolved problems][7] in [number theory][4] and all of [mathematics][8]. It states that every [even][9] [natural number][10] greater than 2 is the sum of two [prime numbers][11].

The conjecture has been shown to hold for all natural numbers less than 4 × 10 18, but remains [unproven][12] despite considerable effort.

## History

[[edit][13]]

### Origins

[[edit][14]]

On 7 June 1742, the [Prussian][15] mathematician [Christian Goldbach][5] wrote a letter to [Leonhard Euler][16] (letter XLIII), [2] in which he proposed the following conjecture:

Every integer that can be written as the sum of two primes can also be written as the sum of as many primes (including unity) as one wishes, until all terms are units. [a]

Goldbach was following the now-abandoned convention of [considering 1][17] to be a [prime number][11], [3] so that a sum of units would be a sum of primes. He then proposed a second conjecture in the margin of his letter, which implies the first: 2 is misprinted as 1 in the marginal conjecture."}},"i":0}}]}'> [b]

It seems at least, that every integer greater than 2 can be written as the sum of three primes. [c]

Euler replied in a letter dated 30 June 1742 [4] and reminded Goldbach of an earlier conversation they had had (*"... so Ew. vormals mit mir communicirt haben..."*), in which Goldbach had remarked that the first of those two conjectures would follow from the statement

Every positive even integer can be written as the sum of two primes.

This is in fact equivalent to his second, marginal conjecture. In the letter dated 30 June 1742, Euler stated: [5] [6]

That... every even integer is a sum of two primes, I regard as a completely certain theorem, although I cannot prove it. [d]

### Weaker Descartes' conjecture

[[edit][18]]

[René Descartes][19] wrote, "Every even number can be expressed as the sum of at most three primes." [7] This proposition is similar to, but weaker than, Goldbach's conjecture. [Paul Erdős][20] said, "Descartes actually discovered this before Goldbach ... but it is better that the conjecture was named for Goldbach because, mathematically speaking, Descartes was infinitely rich and Goldbach was very poor." [8]

### Partial results

[[edit][21]]

Goldbach's conjecture involving the sum of two primes is much more difficult than the [weak Goldbach conjecture][22], which says that every odd integer greater than 5 is the sum of three primes. Using [Vinogradov's method][23], [Nikolai Chudakov][24], [9] [Johannes van der Corput][25], [10] and [Theodor Estermann][26] [11] showed (1937–1938) that [almost all][27] even numbers can be written as the sum of two primes (in the sense that the fraction of even numbers up to some N which can be so written tends towards 1 as N increases). In 1930, [Lev Schnirelmann][28] proved that any [natural number][10] greater than 1 can be written as the sum of not more than C prime numbers, where C is an effectively computable constant; see [Schnirelmann density][29]. [12] [13] Schnirelmann's constant is the lowest number C with this property. Schnirelmann himself obtained *C*< 800 000. This result was subsequently enhanced by many authors, such as [Olivier Ramaré][30], who in 1995 showed that every even number *n*≥ 4 is in fact the sum of at most 6 primes. The best known result stems from the proof of the weak Goldbach conjecture by [Harald Helfgott][31], [14] which directly implies that every even number *n*≥ 4 is the sum of at most 4 primes. [15] [16]

In 1924, [Hardy][32] and [Littlewood][33] showed [17] under the assumption of the [generalized Riemann hypothesis][34] that the number of even numbers up to X violating the Goldbach conjecture is [much less than][35] {{1/2}} + ''c''</sup>"}},"i":0}}]}'>*X*1 ⁄ 2 + *c*for small c. [18]

In 1948, using [sieve theory][36] methods, [Alfréd Rényi][37] showed that every sufficiently large even number can be written as the sum of a prime and an [almost prime][38] with at most K factors. [19] [Chen Jingrun][39] showed in 1973 using sieve theory that every [sufficiently large][40] even number can be written as the sum of either two primes, or a prime and a [semiprime][41] (the product of two primes). [20] See [Chen's theorem][42] for further information.

In 1975, [Hugh Lowell Montgomery][43] and [Bob Vaughan][44] showed that "most" even numbers are expressible as the sum of two primes. More precisely, they showed that there exist positive constants c and C such that for all sufficiently large numbers N, every even number less than N is the sum of two primes, with at most 1 − ''c''</sup>"}},"i":0}}]}'>*CN*1 − *c*exceptions. In particular, the set of even integers that are not the sum of two primes has [density][45] zero.

In 1951, [Yuri Linnik][46] proved the existence of a constant K such that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of two primes and at most K powers of 2. [János Pintz][47] and [Imre Ruzsa][48] found in 2020 that *K*= 8 works. [21] Assuming the [generalized Riemann hypothesis][34], *K*= 7 also works, as shown by [Roger Heath-Brown][49] and [Jan-Christoph Schlage-Puchta][50] in 2002. [22]

In 2013, [Harald Helfgott][31] submitted a proof of the weak conjecture to the *[Annals of Mathematics Studies][51]*book series. The article was accepted, but Helfgott decided to undertake major modifications suggested by the referee. [23] [24] [25] The weak conjecture is implied by the Goldbach conjecture, as if *n*− 3 is a sum of two primes, then n is a sum of three primes. But the converse implication and thus the Goldbach conjecture remain unproven even if Helfgott's proof is correct.

### Computational results

[[edit][52]]

For small values of n, the Goldbach conjecture (and hence the weak Goldbach conjecture) can be verified directly. For instance, in 1938, Nils Pipping laboriously verified the conjecture up to *n*= 100 000. [26] With the advent of computers, many more values of n have been checked; T. Oliveira e Silva ran a distributed computer search that has verified the conjecture for *n*≤ 4 × 10 18 (and double-checked up to 4 × 10 17) as of 2013. One record from this search is that 3 325 581 707 333 960 528 is the smallest number that cannot be written as a sum of two primes where one is smaller than 9781. [27]

## Formal statement

[[edit][53]]

Each of the three conjectures has a natural analog in terms of the modern definition of a prime, under which 1 is excluded. A modern version of the first conjecture is:

Every integer that can be written as the sum of two primes can also be written as the sum of as many primes as one wishes, until either all terms are two (if the integer is even) or one term is three and all other terms are two (if the integer is odd).

A modern version of the marginal conjecture is:

Every integer greater than 5 can be written as the sum of three primes.

And a modern version of Goldbach's older conjecture of which Euler reminded him is:

Every even integer greater than 2 can be written as the sum of two primes.

These modern versions might not be entirely equivalent to the corresponding original statements. For example, if there were an even integer *N*= *p*+ 1 larger than 4, for p a prime, that could not be expressed as the sum of two primes in the modern sense, then it would be a counterexample to the modern version of the third conjecture (without being a counterexample to the original version). The modern version is thus probably stronger (but in order to confirm that, one would have to prove that the first version, freely applied to any positive even integer n, could not possibly rule out the existence of such a specific counterexample N). In any case, the modern statements have the same relationships with each other as the older statements did. That is, the second and third modern statements are equivalent, and either implies the first modern statement.

The third modern statement (equivalent to the second) is the form in which the conjecture is usually expressed today. It is also known as the " [strong][54] ", "even", or "binary" Goldbach conjecture. A weaker form of the second modern statement, known as " [Goldbach's weak conjecture][6] ", the "odd Goldbach conjecture", or the "ternary Goldbach conjecture", asserts that

Every odd integer greater than 7 can be written as the sum of three odd primes.

## Heuristic justification

[[edit][55]]

Statistical considerations that focus on the [probabilistic distribution of prime numbers][57] present informal evidence in favour of the conjecture (in both the weak and strong forms) for [sufficiently large][40] integers: the greater the integer, the more ways there are available for that number to be represented as the sum of two or three other numbers and the more "likely" it becomes that at least one of these representations consists entirely of primes.

## Goldbach partition function

[[edit][71]]

[72] Goldbach's comet; red, blue and green points correspond respectively the values 0, 1 and 2 modulo 3 of the number.

The *Goldbach partition function*associates to each even integer the number of ways it can be decomposed into a sum of two primes. Its graph looks like a [comet][73] and is therefore called [Goldbach's comet][74]. [28]

Goldbach's comet suggests tight upper and lower bounds on the number of representations of an even number as the sum of two primes, and also that the number of these representations depends strongly on the value modulo 3 of the number.

## Related problems

[[edit][75]]

Although Goldbach's conjecture implies that every positive integer greater than one can be written as a sum of at most three primes, it is not always possible to find such a sum using a [greedy algorithm][76] that uses the largest possible prime at each step. The [Pillai sequence][77] tracks the numbers requiring the largest number of primes in their greedy representations. [29]

Similar problems to Goldbach's conjecture exist in which primes are replaced by other particular sets of numbers, such as the squares:

- [Lagrange][78] proved that [every positive integer is the sum of four squares][79]. See [Waring's problem][80] and the related [Waring–Goldbach problem][81] on sums of powers of primes.
- Hardy and Littlewood listed as their Conjecture I: "Every large odd number ( 5"}},"i":0}}]}'>*n*> 5) is the sum of a prime and the double of a prime". [30] This conjecture is known as [Lemoine's conjecture][82] and is also called *Levy's conjecture*.
- The Goldbach conjecture for [practical numbers][83], a prime-like sequence of integers, was stated by Margenstern in 1984, [31] and proved by [Melfi][84] in 1996: [32] every even number is a sum of two practical numbers.
- [Harvey Dubner][85] proposed a strengthening of the Goldbach conjecture: that every even integer greater than 4208 is the sum of two [twin primes][70] (not necessarily belonging to the same pair). [33] [*[better source needed][86]*] Only 34 even integers less than 4208 are not the sum of two twin primes; Dubner has verified computationally that this list is complete up to 2 ⋅ 10 10. {\displaystyle 2\cdot 10^{10}.}[image: {\displaystyle 2\cdot 10^{10}.}] [34] [*[verification needed][87]*] A proof of this stronger conjecture would imply not only Goldbach's conjecture but also the [twin prime conjecture][88].

Goldbach's conjecture is used to study computational complexity. [35] The connection is made through the [Busy Beaver][89] function, where BB(*n*) is the maximum number of steps taken by any *n*state [Turing machine][90] that halts. There is a 27-state Turing machine that halts if and only if Goldbach's conjecture is false. [35] Hence if BB(27) was known, and the Turing machine did not stop in that number of steps, it would be known to run forever and hence no counterexamples would exist (proving the conjecture). This is a completely impractical way to settle the conjecture; instead it is used to suggest that BB(27) will be very hard to compute, at least as difficult as settling the Goldbach conjecture.
