###### Abstract

In 1999, Jacobson and Lehel conjectured that for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3, every k k -regular Hamiltonian graph has cycles of at least linearly many different lengths. This was further strengthened by Verstraëte, who asked whether the regularity can be replaced with the weaker condition that the minimum degree is at least 3 3. Despite attention from various researchers, until now, the best partial result towards both of these conjectures was a n \sqrt{n} lower bound on the number of cycle lengths. We resolve these conjectures asymptotically, by showing that the number of cycle lengths is at least n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)}.

## 1 Introduction

The study of cycles in graphs goes back to the early days of graph theory and has been fundamental ever since. Of particular interest are Hamilton cycles, i.e. cycles passing through all the vertices of a graph. Starting with the cornerstone theorem of Dirac [7], there are many results giving sufficient conditions for a graph to be Hamiltonian, for some other classical examples see [4, 5, 6, 11, 19]. In 1973, Bondy [3] made the “meta-conjecture” that any non-trivial condition which guarantees the existence of a Hamilton cycle, should also guarantee that the given graph is pancyclic, i.e. contains cycles of all possible lengths, with possibly a simple family of exceptions. This assertion turned out to be influential, and by now there are numerous appealing results of this type. For example, Bondy himself [2] proved that Ore’s sufficient condition for Hamiltonicity (that the sum of degrees of any pair of non-adjacent vertices is at least n n), implies that the graph is either pancyclic or isomorphic to the complete bipartite graph K n / 2, n / 2 K_{n/2,n/2}. Bauer and Schmeichel [1], relying on previous results of Schmeichel and Hakimi [20], have shown that the sufficient conditions for Hamiltonicity of Bondy [4], Chvátal [5] and Fan [11] all imply pancyclicity, barring a small family of exceptions. Jackson and Ordaz [15] conjectured that any graph G G whose connectivity κ ⁡ ( G) \kappa(G) is strictly larger than its independence number α ⁡ ( G) \alpha(G) must be pancyclic. This conjecture is motivated by the classical theorem of Chvátal and Erdős [6] that a graph with κ ⁡ ( G) ≥ α ⁡ ( G) \kappa(G)\geq\alpha(G) must be Hamiltonian. An approximate form of the conjecture was proven by Keevash and Sudakov [16], who showed that κ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 600 ​ α ​ ( G) \kappa(G)\geq 600\alpha(G) is already sufficient for pancyclicity.

Pancyclicity is just an instance of a wider class of problems, which study the properties of the set of cycle lengths of a graph with connection to other graph parameters. The set of cycle lengths of G G is called its cycle spectrum, and denoted 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) \mathcal{C}(G). There are by now numerous results relating properties of 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) \mathcal{C}(G) to various graph parameters. For example, Erdős [9] conjectured that a graph G G with girth g g and average degree d d must satisfy | 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) | ≥ Ω ⁡ ( d ⌊ g − 1 2 ⌋) |\mathcal{C}(G)|\geq\Omega(d^{\lfloor\frac{g-1}{2}\rfloor}). The case g = 5 g=5 was settled by Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau, and Schelp [10]. Later, Sudakov and Verstraëte [21] proved the full conjecture in a strong form. Another example is a result of Gould, Haxell and Scott [14] that a graph with minimum degree c ​ n cn must have a cycle of any even length between 4 4 and e ​ c ​ ( G) − K ec(G)-K, where e ​ c ​ ( G) ec(G) is the length of a longest even cycle in G G and K K is a constant depending only on c c. We should also mention the recent work of Gao, Huo, Liu and Ma [12], who proved several conjectures relating properties of 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) \mathcal{C}(G) to the minimum degree, connectivity or chromatic number of G G.

Bondy’s meta-conjecture is about conditions for Hamiltonicity which imply pancyclicity. A natural question in the opposite direction is as follows: Let us assume that a graph G G is Hamiltonian; under which assumptions can we also guarantee that G G is pancyclic? Since pancyclicity is sometimes too strong of a requirement, we can relax it and ask to find many cycle lengths. Questions of this type were first introduced by Jacobson and Lehel at the 1999 conference “Paul Erdős and His Mathematics”, where they asked for the minimum size of the cycle spectrum of a k k -regular Hamiltonian graph G G on n n vertices? The aforementioned result of Bondy [2] implies that if k = ⌈ n / 2 ⌉ k=\lceil n/2\rceil, then G G is pancyclic unless G = K n / 2, n / 2 G=K_{n/2,n/2}. At the other extreme, if k = 2 k=2 then G G clearly has just one cycle. Jacobson and Lehel conjectured that already for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3, the number of cycle lengths should be linear in n n. This is best possible, since they also observed that one cannot expect to have pancyclicity. Indeed, assuming 2 ​ k 2k divides n n, take n 2 ​ k \frac{n}{2k} disjoint copies of K k, k K_{k,k}, ordered in a cycle, remove an edge from each of them, and add an edge between any two consecutive copies such that the resulting graph is k k -regular. It is not hard to see that in this construction, the possible cycle lengths are precisely all the even integers between 4 4 and 2 ​ k 2k and between 2 ​ n k \frac{2n}{k} and n n. This gives in total n 2 ⋅ k − 2 k + k \frac{n}{2}\cdot\frac{k-2}{k}+k different lengths.

Soon after the above question was first circulated, Gould, Jacobson and Pfender proved that | 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) | ≥ Ω ⁡ ( n) |\mathcal{C}(G)|\geq\Omega(\sqrt{n}) for every k k -regular n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph G G (with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3). This bound was subsequently obtained by several other authors. Yet, prior to our work, the n \sqrt{n} bound was the best known result. In particular, Girão, Kittipassorn and Narayanan [13] remarked that improving this estimate would be of considerable interest. Furthermore, the following strengthening of the above conjecture of Jacobson and Lehel, which replaces the k k -regularity condition with the assumption that the minimum degree is at least 3 3, was proposed by Verstraëte [25].

###### Conjecture 1.

An n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph G G with δ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 3 \delta(G)\geq 3 has Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(n) different cycle lengths.

While the special case of this conjecture for regular graphs already seems quite challenging, it is natural to expect that the full Conjecture 1 is even harder. The reason for this is that often problems become more difficult when the regularity requirement is replaced by a minimum degree assumption. One well-known example is a conjecture of Thomassen [22], that a graph with a large enough minimum degree contains a subgraph of large minimum degree and large girth. This conjecture is open even for girth 7 7. However, this statement becomes easy if the given graph is regular, see e.g. [18]. Such situations arise also for questions related to the one studied here: A classical result of Smith (see [24] and also [23]) states that every Hamiltonian 3 3 -regular graph G G contains a second Hamilton cycle. As was shown by Entringer and Swart [8], this is no longer true if instead of 3 3 -regularity we assume that δ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 3 \delta(G)\geq 3 (even if all degrees are equal to 3 3 or 4 4). Girão, Kittipassorn and Narayanan [13] required an involved proof to even show that a Hamiltonian G G with δ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 3 \delta(G)\geq 3 contains a second cycle of length at least n − o ⁡ ( n) n-o(n). In contrast, for regular G G, this proof can be simplified considerably and gives a better bound.

It is worth noting that if one replaces the minimum degree requirement δ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 3 \delta(G)\geq 3, with the requirement that the average degree is at least 3 3, then the aforementioned lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) is tight. More generally, Milans, Pfender, Rautenbach, Regen and West [17] have shown that a graph G G with n n vertices and m m edges satisfies | 𝒞 ⁡ ( G) | ≥ ( 1 − o ⁡ ( 1)) ​ m − n |\mathcal{C}(G)|\geq(1-o(1))\sqrt{m-n}, and this is tight.

In this paper, we prove the following theorem, which resolves Conjecture 1 asymptotically:

###### Theorem 2.

An n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph G G with δ ⁡ ( G) ≥ 3 \delta(G)\geq 3 contains cycles of n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} different lengths.

Our proof of Theorem 2 is constructive: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding cycles of n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} different lengths in a Hamiltonian graph of minimum degree 3 3, provided a Hamilton cycle is specified.

## 2 A sketch and main ideas

The most general overarching idea that we employ is to split the Hamilton cycle into pieces (usually paths or pairs of paths) and then find paths with lengths on a different “scale” in different parts. To illustrate what we mean, let us consider the following situation. Suppose that we managed to split our Hamilton cycle into two paths P 1, P 2 P_{1},P_{2}, such that there are still many chords inside the vertex-set of each P i P_{i} (or, more precisely, that inside each P i P_{i} there is a linear number of vertices touching a chord whose other endpoint is also on P i P_{i}). Suppose that we found k = Ω ⁡ ( n) k=\Omega(\sqrt{n}) paths Q 1, …, Q k Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k} between the endpoints of P 1 P_{1} (which only use the vertices of P 1 P_{1}), such that | Q 1 |, …, | Q k | |Q_{1}|,\dots,|Q_{k}| are all different and all belong to an interval of width n \sqrt{n}. Suppose further that we found ℓ = Ω ⁡ ( n) \ell=\Omega(\sqrt{n}) paths R 1, …, R ℓ R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell} between the endpoints of P 2 P_{2} (which only use the vertices of P 2 P_{2}), such that the lengths of any two of these paths are at least n \sqrt{n} apart, namely, | | R i | − | R j | | > n ||R_{i}|-|R_{j}||>\sqrt{n} for all i ≠ j i\neq j. In this situation, we can combine any one of the Q i Q_{i} ’s with any one the R j R_{j} ’s, joining them into a cycle of length | Q i | + | R j | |Q_{i}|+|R_{j}|. The crucial point is that the k ​ ℓ k\ell numbers | Q i | + | R j | |Q_{i}|+|R_{j}| are all different. In other words, we use the “condensed” lengths Q 1, …, Q k Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k} to “fill in the gaps” between the “spread-out” lengths R 1, …, R ℓ R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell} (see 4 for the details on this). In total, this would give us k ​ ℓ = Ω ⁡ ( n) k\ell=\Omega(n) different cycle lengths. Hence, achieving both above goals would establish Conjecture 1.

We believe that both above statements should be true, namely, that one can find both Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) distinct path lengths all contained in an interval of width n \sqrt{n} and Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) path lengths which are n \sqrt{n} apart. Observe that both of these statements are essentially implied by 1, and that our main result shows that both hold asymptotically (i.e., with n \sqrt{n} replaced by n 1 / 2 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1/2-o(1)}). On the other hand, these statements shift the difficulty from finding many lengths (note that there have been a number of proofs that there are at least n \sqrt{n} different lengths over the years) to controlling what kind of lengths we find.

Our actual strategy for tackling Conjecture 1 is a bit more involved. Instead of splitting our cycle into just two parts, we split it into a larger number k k of parts (with k k to be chosen as roughly log ⁡ n \sqrt{\log n}). Here each part will be a pair of cycle sections (subpaths of the cycle) with at least n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} chords between them, with different section-pairs situated “on top of” each other (see Figure 3). Now, with the goal of finding n 1 − ε n^{1-\varepsilon} different lengths (where ε \varepsilon is an appropriately chosen vanishing function of n n), we shall proceed as follows. Inside the first of the k k parts, we shall find Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) path lengths all belonging to an interval of width n ε n^{\varepsilon}. Then, inside the second part, we shall find about Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) lengths ℓ 1 < … < ℓ t \ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{t} such that any two consecutive lengths are Θ ⁡ ( n ε) \Theta(n^{\varepsilon}) apart, namely ℓ i + 1 − ℓ i = Θ ⁡ ( n ε) \ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i}=\Theta(n^{\varepsilon}) for all i i. Now, by combining the paths we found in these two parts, we will get Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \Omega(n^{2\varepsilon}) different path lengths, all belonging to an interval of width O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) O(n^{2\varepsilon}), and only using vertices from the first two parts of the partition. Continuing in this manner, we will find inside the third part Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) lengths which are Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \Theta(n^{2\varepsilon}) apart, inside the fourth part Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) lengths which are Θ ⁡ ( n 3 ​ ε) \Theta(n^{3\varepsilon}) apart, and so on. This will always allow us to combine the new lengths we find with the lengths found so far to get Ω ⁡ ( n i ​ ε) \Omega(n^{i\varepsilon}) different path lengths, all belonging to an interval of width O ⁡ ( n i ​ ε) O(n^{i\varepsilon}), only using vertices from the first i i parts. In each iteration we will actually lose a polylogarithmic factor in the number of paths we find, which will result in the optimal number of iterations being log ⁡ n log ⁡ log ⁡ n \sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}} (this corresponds to having ε = log ⁡ log ⁡ n log ⁡ n \varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}}). After this number of iterations, we will find n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} different lengths. This iterative process is handled in 13.

Let us now focus on a single iteration and sketch the main ideas involved. For simplicity, suppose that this is the third iteration, namely, that our goal is to find (inside the third of the k k parts of the partition) Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) path lengths ℓ 1 < … < ℓ t \ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{t} with ℓ i + 1 − ℓ i = Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i}=\Theta(n^{2\varepsilon}) for all i i. Up to this step, we have already found Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \Theta(n^{2\varepsilon}) path lengths in an interval of width O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) O(n^{2\varepsilon}) inside the first two parts. Now, we consider a maximum collection e 1, …, e m e_{1},\dots,e_{m} of chords inside the third part, such that for all i ≠ j i\neq j, the lengths of e i e_{i} and e j e_{j} differ by at least n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}. Each chord e i e_{i} gives rise to a path inside the third part (namely, the path that consists of the chord and pieces of the cycle), and the lengths of any two of these m m paths differ by at least n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}. Now, observe that if m ≥ n 1 − 3 ​ ε m\geq n^{1-3\varepsilon}, then by combining these paths with the Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \Theta(n^{2\varepsilon}) path lengths we found in the first two parts of the partition, we obtain altogether m ⋅ Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) = Ω ⁡ ( n 1 − ε) m\cdot\Omega(n^{2\varepsilon})=\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon}) different cycle lengths, and thus achieve our goal already at this stage. So we may assume that m ≤ n 1 − 3 ​ ε m\leq n^{1-3\varepsilon}. Since e 1, …, e m e_{1},\dots,e_{m} is a maximal family, the length of any other chord must be at distance at most n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon} to that of one of the e i e_{i} ’s. By averaging (and as each part of the partition contains n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} chords), we see that there is a family E E of at least n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) / m ≥ n 3 ​ ε − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)}/m\geq n^{3\varepsilon-o(1)} different chords, whose lengths all belong to an interval of width n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}. The reason such a family E E is useful is as follows: Suppose we partition the two cycle sections of the third part into subpaths X 1, X 2, … X_{1},X_{2},\ldots of length n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}. Then, for any two such subpaths X i, X j X_{i},X_{j} on the left side, which are not consecutive (and hence are at distance larger than n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon} on the path), any chord touching X 1 X_{1} must interlace (i.e. cross) any chord touching X 2 X_{2}. For if not, then the difference of the lengths of these two chords is larger than n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}, contradicting the fact that both lengths belong to an interval of width n 2 ​ ε n^{2\varepsilon}. So we see that E E decomposes into pairwise-interlacing pieces, see Figure 3 for an illustration. This structure, together with some additional arguments, then allows us to find the Ω ⁡ ( n ε) \Omega(n^{\varepsilon}) desired path lengths ℓ 1 < … < ℓ t \ell_{1}<\ldots<\ell_{t}. We remark that while it is not hard to find such lengths with ℓ i + 1 − ℓ i = Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i}=\Omega(n^{2\varepsilon}), which already allows us to find Ω ⁡ ( n 3 ​ ε) \Omega(n^{3\varepsilon}) lengths, it is essential for the next iteration that these lengths are not too far apart, in other words ensuring in addition that ℓ i + 1 − ℓ i ≤ O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ ε) \ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i}\leq O(n^{2\varepsilon}) is crucial in order to be able to continue our argument.

Executing the above strategy presents significant technical difficulties, in part due to the need to join the various pieces in suitable ways. This sometimes makes various parts of the argument a bit cumbersome, and we try to alleviate this situation with a number of figures illustrating the argument.

Finally, we note that even splitting the cycle into pieces (i.e. subpaths or pairs of sections), a preliminary step for the above strategy, is non-trivial and requires some work. To illustrate this, note that if our graph only consisted of “diameter” chords (joining vertices which are as far apart on the cycle as possible), then it is not possible to split the cycle into two paths with each of them still containing many chords. The way we go around this issue is to show that one can “reroute” the original Hamilton cycle using two chords (and large parts of the Hamilton cycle), thus obtaining a new cycle which admits the desired split.

## 5 Concluding remarks

In this paper, we proved that an n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph of minimum degree 3 3 has cycles of n 1 − o ⁡ ( 1) n^{1-o(1)} different lengths, which shows that Conjecture 1 holds asymptotically. Moreover, for the original question of Jacobson and Lehel (dealing with graphs of bounded degree), we can use our ideas to get a better quantitative bound of n polylog ​ ( n) \frac{n}{\text{polylog}(n)}. Still, it would be very interesting to prove a linear bound on the number of cycles, even in the 3 3 -regular case. Towards this, we propose the following natural intermediate steps:

###### Conjecture 15.

In an n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph with minimum degree 3 3, there are:

1. 1.

Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) cycle lengths all belonging to an interval of size O ⁡ ( n) O(\sqrt{n}).

2. 2.

Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) cycle lengths any two of which are at least Ω ⁡ ( n) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) apart.

Observe first that Conjecture 1 immediately implies Conjecture 15. On the other hand, a slight strengthening of Conjecture 15 already implies Conjecture 1. Indeed, suppose that in Conjecture 15, we replace the assumption of minimum degree 3 3 with the assumption that linearly many vertices have degree at least 3 3. Furthermore, suppose that instead of starting with a Hamilton cycle we start with a Hamilton path, and instead of cycle lengths we consider path lengths of paths going between the endpoints of the given Hamilton path. Then by splitting our Hamilton cycle (or a “rerouted” cycle) into two paths (similarly to what is done in the proof of Theorem 14), we can apply this strengthened version of Conjecture 15 and obtain linearly many cycle lengths (see Section 2 for some details).

Finally, let us also discuss the following related question, which was already mentioned in passing in the introduction. What is the minimum C C such that every n n -vertex Hamiltonian graph with minimum degree 3 3 contains a second cycle of length at least n − C n-C? Girão, Kittipassorn and Narayanan [13] show that C = O ⁡ ( n 4 / 5) = o ⁡ ( n) C=O(n^{4/5})=o(n), and conjecture that actually C = O ⁡ ( 1) C=O(1). It is also interesting to ask whether the answer changes if we want in addition to have C > 0 C>0 (i.e., if we forbid this second cycle from being a Hamilton cycle). Such a result might be useful to attack the conjecture of Jacobson and Lehel.
