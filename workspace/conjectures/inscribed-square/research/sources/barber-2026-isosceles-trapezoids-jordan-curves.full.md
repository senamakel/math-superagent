Inscriptions of Isosceles Trapezoids in Jordan Curves

Adam Barber

May 1, 2026

Abstract

We construct a Lagrangian Floer homology whose chain complex is generically generated by the
inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in a smooth Jordan curve. This is an extension of Greene and
Lobb’s Jordan Floer homology [8], which we also call Jordan Floer homology. Its non-triviality re-
establishes that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid. By consideration
of the spectral invariants associated with the real filtration known as the action filtration, we
establish new cases of non-smooth Jordan curves which admit inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids.

1 Introduction

We say that a Jordan curve, a simple closed curve γ ⊂ R2, inscribes a square if there exist 4 points
in γ ⊂ R
2 that are the vertices of a square. The classical example of an inscription problem is the
Square Peg Problem, which was first posed by Toeplitz [18] in 1911 and asks whether every Jordan
curve inscribes a square.

Conjecture 1.1 (Square Peg Problem). Every Jordan curve inscribes a square.

This remains an open problem. More generally, we say that a Jordan curve γ inscribes a polygon P
if there exist 4 points in γ that are the vertices of a polygon similar to P (we say that two polygons
are similar if there exists an orientation preserving similarity between them). We consider inscriptions
of isosceles trapezoids (quadrilaterals whose vertices are the intersection set of two parallel lines and
a circle) in Jordan curves. As a generalisation of the Square Peg Problem, it is also an open question
whether every Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

Conjecture 1.2 (Isosceles Trapezoid Peg Problem). Every Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles
trapezoid.

In [8], Greene and Lobb developed a Lagrangian Floer homology associated with inscriptions of
rectangles in smooth Jordan curves, called Jordan Floer homology. The goal of this paper is to
define and study Jordan Floer homology in the setting of inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in smooth
Jordan curves. The non-triviality of this homology for smooth curves provides an alternative proof of
the already established result:

Theorem 1.3 (Greene-Lobb, 2020 [7]). Every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

Their result was in fact somewhat stronger and established that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes
every cyclic quadrilateral (quadrilaterals that inscribe in a circle).

An advantage of the Jordan Floer approach over the approach used in [7] to show that every smooth
Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid is the existence of spectral invariants, which are related
to the areas of inscribed isosceles trapezoids. One way to consider inscriptions of an isosceles trapezoid
T in a non-smooth Jordan curve γ is to consider inscriptions of T in smooth Jordan curves γn limiting
to γ and use this to conclude that γ must inscribe T . However, this approach suffers from the issue of
shrinkout, which we will describe precisely in Section 1.1.1.
In the case of piecewise C 1 Jordan curves without cusps, the issue of shrinkout can be obstructed
by elementary techniques. This was observed by Matschke [12, Section 5.3] and when combined with
Theorem 1.3 establishes the following result.

Theorem 1.4 (Matschke, 2021 [12]). Every piecewise C 1 Jordan curve without cusps inscribes every
isosceles trapezoid.

For broader classes of Jordan curves, such as rectifiable (finite length) or locally monotone (see Def-
inition 1.9) curves, this approach breaks down. In such cases, the spectral invariants provide an
alternative approach to obstruct shrinkout, enabling us to deduce the existence of inscribed isosceles
trapezoids for certain new classes of non-smooth Jordan curves.
The approach of using spectral invariants to obstruct shrinkout was introduced in [8] in the case
of rectangles. Analysis of the spectral invariants in the case of non-rectangular isosceles trapezoids is
made harder due to less control over the limiting behaviour of the spectral invariants. To illuminate
this issue, consider a sequence of inscribed isosceles trapezoids in a smooth Jordan curve γ such that
the angle between the diagonals of the isosceles trapezoid tends to π. Limiting to the case where all
the inscribed isosceles trapezoids are rectangles, the limit is necessarily a binormal (a line segment
normal to γ at both ends). However, without this restriction, the limit could also be a vertex (a critical
point of curvature of γ) or a quadrisecant (a line segment that intersects γ in at least 4 points). The
potential existence of quadrisecants in this limit (see Figure 7 for example) is what makes the isosceles
trapezoid case harder.
An example of a class of curves for which we can control this limit is locally Lipschitz-graphical
Jordan curves (see Definition 1.10), which form a subset of rectifiable locally monotone Jordan curves
and contains all piecewise C 1 Jordan curves without cusps. Isosceles trapezoids can be parameterised
by two parameters: the angle between the diagonal θ, and an aspect ratio r characterising where the
diagonals intersect (see Section 1.2). We now provide a sample result for this class of curves.

Theorem A. Let γ be a locally Lipschitz-graphical Jordan curve bounding an area Area(γ) and of
radius Rad(γ), then γ inscribes an isosceles trapezoid of angle θ and aspect ratio r for every

The final result of note is that of Asano and Ike [1], who recently established that every rectifiable
or locally monotone Jordan curve inscribes every rectangle. Their work was inspired by the techniques
of Greene and Lobb [8], but used microlocal sheaf theory in place of Lagrangian Floer homology.

Theorem 1.5 (Asano-Ike, 2026 [1]). Let γ be a rectifiable or locally monotone Jordan curve, then γ
inscribes every rectangle.

However, their approach relies on the associated Lagrangians being monotone, a property that fails to
hold in the setting of isosceles trapezoids. It is therefore not clear how their methods could be adapted
to address the problem of inscribing isosceles trapezoids.

1.1 Why isosceles trapezoids?

To motivate our interest in inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids, as opposed to any other class of quadri-
laterals, we consider the following result for cyclic quadrilaterals.

Theorem 1.6 (Greene-Lobb, 2020 [7]). Every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every cyclic quadrilateral.

This theorem is optimal in two ways: any quadrilateral that is not cyclic does not inscribe in a circle
(which is a smooth Jordan curve); and for any cyclic quadrilateral that is not an isosceles trapezoid
there exists a non-smooth Jordan curve (in fact a triangle) in which it does not inscribe. This both
motivates our interest in isosceles trapezoids, as the widest class of quadrilaterals for which the problem
of inscription on general Jordan curves remains open, and demonstrates an obstruction to progress on
the square peg problem: the issue of shrinkout.

1.1.1 The issue of shrinkout

Suppose that γ is a continuous Jordan curve in C. The Riemann mapping theorem tells us that there
exists a biholomorphic map f : D → D, where D = {z ∈ C : |z| < 1} and D is the bounded region of
C \ γ. Carath´eodory’s extension to the Riemann mapping theorem tells us that this map continuously
extends to a homeomorphism f : D → D. The Jordan curve γ is therefore the limit of a sequence of
smooth Jordan curves γn, the images of concentric circles in D approaching the unit circle ∂D from
the interior.
Fix a cyclic quadrilateral Q. From Theorem 1.6, we know that each smooth Jordan curve γn
inscribes any given cyclic quadrilateral, so we get a sequence of inscriptions Qn ⊂ γn where Qn is
an inscription of Q in γn. Compactness ensures that there exists a convergent subsequence Qni of
inscriptions of Q. It is therefore tempting to conclude that γ must inscribe Q. However, in the limit
the inscription of Q may shrink to a single point. From the discussion under Theorem 1.6, we know
that this is necessarily the case when Q is a cyclic quadrilateral that is not an isosceles trapezoid and
the limiting curve γ is a triangle that does not inscribe Q. We call this phenomenon shrinkout.
To approach the issue of shrinkout, we use the same approach as [8, 9], that is, we use a version of
Lagrangian Floer homology called Jordan Floer homology, which has an associated spectral invariant.
We will show that this spectral invariant relates to an area associated with the inscribed isosceles
trapezoids, so controlling the variation of this spectral invariant can be used in special cases to obstruct
shrinkout.

1.3 Jordan Floer homology

The approach to construct Jordan Floer homology associated with inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids
will be broadly the same as that used by Greene and Lobb for the case of rectangles in [8], now with
an extra parameter r. Together, the aspect ratio r and angle θ parametrise the space of isosceles
trapezoids up to similarity (as described above) with the case r = 1/2 representing rectangles. We
now provide an outline of the approach, omitting technical details.
Given a Jordan curve γ ⊂ C, we consider the torus γ × γ ⊂ C2. This torus is Lagrangian in C
2

with respect to the symplectic form ω = (1 − r) · dx1 ∧ dy1 + r · dx2 ∧ dy2. We then define a Hamiltonian
H such that the time-1 flow of the associated Hamiltonian vector field XH is the transformation Gr,θ.
This gives a second Lagrangian torus Gr,θ(γ × γ). Generically, these Lagrangian tori intersect cleanly
along the loop ∆(γ) and transversally at points of intersection away from this loop, which we call non-
trivial intersection points and represent inscribed isosceles trapezoids. To each of these intersection
points p, there is an associated trajectory τ : [0, 1] → C
2 of the Hamiltonian vector field XH with
τ (0) ∈ γ × γ and τ (1) = p. Non-trivial intersection points are in one-to-one correspondence with
non-constant trajectories.
We then construct a chain complex, the Jordan Floer chain complex JFC(γ, r, θ), generically gen-
erated by the non-constant trajectories of H. The differential ∂ on this chain complex is defined as
a count of certain strips between trajectories, called pseudo-holomorphic strips. We only consider
pseudo-holomorphic strips that avoid the diagonal ∆(C) = {(z, z) | z ∈ C}. Showing that this dif-
ferential satisfies ∂2 = 0 is one of the key technical steps in defining Jordan Floer homology, as it
requires proving that the limits of diagonal-avoiding pseudo-holomorphic strips stay away from the
diagonal. Fortunately, some of the arguments for establishing Jordan Floer homology for inscriptions
of rectangles made in [8] extend directly to the isosceles trapezoid case. Taking the homology of the
Jordan Floer chain complex gives the Jordan Floer homology JF(γ, r, θ). Since generators of the chain
complex correspond to inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids of aspect ratio r and angle θ, showing that
JF(γ, r, θ) is non-trivial is sufficient to show that γ inscribes such an isosceles trapezoid.
The Jordan Floer chain complex has a real filtration called the action, which lifts to a filtration
grading for each non-zero homology class α ∈ JF(γ, r, θ) called the spectral invariant of α. There
is a single non-zero homology class in JF2(γ, r, θ), whose associated spectral invariant we denote by
l2(γ, r, θ). This spectral invariant can be used to obstruct shrinkout when considering non-smooth
Jordan curve limits (see Section 1.1.1).

2.2 Jordan Floer chain complex

One issue we wish to avoid is that the Lagrangians may not intersect transversely away from the
diagonal. To achieve transversality, we introduce a small Hamiltonian perturbation. In a small neigh-
bourhood of the diagonal ∆(γ) = {(z, z) | z ∈ γ}, we can ensure that γ × γ and Φ1
Ht(γ × γ) only
intersect at the diagonal, and hence we do not require a Hamiltonian perturbation in this region. This

Definition 2.6 (Width). The width of a smooth Jordan curve γ, denoted Widthr,θ(γ) is the infimal
diagonal length of an inscribed isosceles trapezoid with fixed aspect ratio r ∈ (0, 1/2] and angle θ ∈ (0, π).

Note that we cannot define the width of a smooth Jordan curve as the infimal diagonal length of an
inscribed isosceles trapezoid (with no restriction on r or θ), as would be analogous to the definition
used in [8] for the Jordan Floer complex associated with rectangles, since for any fixed r ̸= 1/2 and
for any smooth Jordan curve γ it is possible to find a sequence of inscribed isosceles trapezoids that
degenerate to a point as θ → π. With our slightly modified definition of width, we now recall some
key definitions from [8] in order to set consistent notation and terminology.

2.5 The continuation package

Currently we require a choice of Hamiltonian perturbation and almost-complex structure in order
to define the Jordan Floer homology. In Lagrangian Floer homology, there is a standard procedure

only the choice of Hamiltonian perturbation ht and almost-complex structure Jt, but also of the angle
θ. A detailed treatment of continuation maps in the more general setting is given in [2, Chapter 11].

3 The symplectic action and spectral invariants

The Jordan Floer homology has an integer homological grading, supported in degrees 1 and 2, called
the Maslov index. The Jordan Floer chain complex also has a real filtration called the action. This lifts
to a filtration grading for each non-zero homology class α ∈ JF(γ, r, θ), called the spectral invariant
of α. Recalling that Jordan Floer homology has a single homology class in each of the degrees it
is supported, we obtain two spectral invariants l1(γ, r, θ) and l2(γ, r, θ). We can use these spectral
invariants to obstruct shrinkout (see Section 1.1.1).
To introduce the symplectic action, we first introduce the space on which it is defined. We define
the path space Ω(C2, γ × γ) as the set of paths σ : [0, 1] → C2 with endpoints in γ × γ. Unfortunately,
the notion of a preferred capping (see Definition 2.11) cannot be extended to all such paths. To deal
with this problem, it is natural to introduce the diagonal avoiding path space

Another important property of spectral invariants is that they satisfy a triangle inequality, which

an important role in the obstruction of shrinkout.

Proof. This follows immediately from the triangle inequality in [11, Theorem 27] once we establish

4 Inscriptions in non-smooth Jordan curves

We now wish to consider inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in non-smooth Jordan curves. As first
introduced in the discussion of the issue of shrinkout (Section 1.1.1), the approach will be to approxi-
mate a non-smooth Jordan curve γ by a sequence of smooth Jordan curves γn. Convergent sequences
of inscribed isosceles trapezoids Tn in the smooth Jordan curves γn limit to some set of points T ⊂ γ.
However, the limiting set T could be a single point, that is, the inscriptions Tn shrinkout. The aim is
therefore to obstruct shrinkout.
We will restrict ourselves to considering rectifiable and locally monotone Jordan curves. Recall
that rectifiable Jordan curves are those of finite length, and locally monotone Jordan curves can be
represented locally as the graph of a continuous function. Such Jordan curves are easier to deal with
since they can be approximated by smooth Jordan curves with nice properties, making it easier to
obstruct shrinkout.
