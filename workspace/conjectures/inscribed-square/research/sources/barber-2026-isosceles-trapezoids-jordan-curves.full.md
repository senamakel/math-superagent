# Inscriptions of Isosceles Trapezoids in Jordan Curves

Adam Barber

###### Abstract

We construct a Lagrangian Floer homology whose chain complex is generically generated by the inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in a smooth Jordan curve. This is an extension of Greene and Lobb’s Jordan Floer homology [8], which we also call Jordan Floer homology. Its non-triviality re-establishes that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid. By consideration of the spectral invariants associated with the real filtration known as the action filtration, we establish new cases of non-smooth Jordan curves which admit inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids.

## 1 Introduction

We say that a Jordan curve, a simple closed curve γ ⊂ ℝ 2 \gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}, *inscribes*a square if there exist 4 points in γ ⊂ ℝ 2 \gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} that are the vertices of a square. The classical example of an inscription problem is the Square Peg Problem, which was first posed by Toeplitz [18] in 1911 and asks whether every Jordan curve inscribes a square.

###### Conjecture 1.1 (Square Peg Problem).

Every Jordan curve inscribes a square.

This remains an open problem. More generally, we say that a Jordan curve γ \gamma*inscribes*a polygon P P if there exist 4 points in γ \gamma that are the vertices of a polygon similar to P P (we say that two polygons are similar if there exists an orientation preserving similarity between them). We consider inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids (quadrilaterals whose vertices are the intersection set of two parallel lines and a circle) in Jordan curves. As a generalisation of the Square Peg Problem, it is also an open question whether every Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

###### Conjecture 1.2 (Isosceles Trapezoid Peg Problem).

Every Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

In [8], Greene and Lobb developed a Lagrangian Floer homology associated with inscriptions of rectangles in smooth Jordan curves, called Jordan Floer homology. The goal of this paper is to define and study Jordan Floer homology in the setting of inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in smooth Jordan curves. The non-triviality of this homology for smooth curves provides an alternative proof of the already established result:

###### Theorem 1.3 (Greene-Lobb, 2020 [7]).

Every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

Their result was in fact somewhat stronger and established that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every cyclic quadrilateral (quadrilaterals that inscribe in a circle).

Figure 1: An example of an inscription of an isosceles trapezoid in a Jordan curve.

An advantage of the Jordan Floer approach over the approach used in [7] to show that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every isosceles trapezoid is the existence of spectral invariants, which are related to the areas of inscribed isosceles trapezoids. One way to consider inscriptions of an isosceles trapezoid T T in a non-smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma is to consider inscriptions of T T in smooth Jordan curves γ n \gamma_{n} limiting to γ \gamma and use this to conclude that γ \gamma must inscribe T T. However, this approach suffers from the issue of *shrinkout*, which we will describe precisely in Section 1.1.1.

In the case of piecewise C 1 C^{1} Jordan curves without cusps, the issue of shrinkout can be obstructed by elementary techniques. This was observed by Matschke [12, Section 5.3] and when combined with Theorem 1.3 establishes the following result.

###### Theorem 1.4 (Matschke, 2021 [12]).

Every piecewise C 1 C^{1} Jordan curve without cusps inscribes every isosceles trapezoid.

For broader classes of Jordan curves, such as rectifiable (finite length) or locally monotone (see Definition 1.9) curves, this approach breaks down. In such cases, the spectral invariants provide an alternative approach to obstruct shrinkout, enabling us to deduce the existence of inscribed isosceles trapezoids for certain new classes of non-smooth Jordan curves.

The approach of using spectral invariants to obstruct shrinkout was introduced in [8] in the case of rectangles. Analysis of the spectral invariants in the case of non-rectangular isosceles trapezoids is made harder due to less control over the limiting behaviour of the spectral invariants. To illuminate this issue, consider a sequence of inscribed isosceles trapezoids in a smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma such that the angle between the diagonals of the isosceles trapezoid tends to π \pi. Limiting to the case where all the inscribed isosceles trapezoids are rectangles, the limit is necessarily a *binormal*(a line segment normal to γ \gamma at both ends). However, without this restriction, the limit could also be a *vertex*(a critical point of curvature of γ \gamma) or a *quadrisecant*(a line segment that intersects γ \gamma in at least 4 points). The potential existence of quadrisecants in this limit (see Figure 7 for example) is what makes the isosceles trapezoid case harder.

An example of a class of curves for which we can control this limit is locally Lipschitz-graphical Jordan curves (see Definition 1.10), which form a subset of rectifiable locally monotone Jordan curves and contains all piecewise C 1 C^{1} Jordan curves without cusps. Isosceles trapezoids can be parameterised by two parameters: the angle between the diagonal θ \theta, and an aspect ratio r r characterising where the diagonals intersect (see Section 1.2). We now provide a sample result for this class of curves.

###### Theorem A.

Let γ \gamma be a locally Lipschitz-graphical Jordan curve bounding an area Area ⁡ ( γ) \operatorname{Area}(\gamma) and of radius Rad ⁡ ( γ) \operatorname{Rad}(\gamma), then γ \gamma inscribes an isosceles trapezoid of angle θ \theta and aspect ratio r r for every

 | 0 < θ < Area ⁡ ( γ) 2 ​ ( 1 − r) ​ Rad ⁡ ( γ) 2. 0<\theta\ <\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\gamma)}{2(1-r)\operatorname{Rad}(\gamma)^{2}}. |  |

The final result of note is that of Asano and Ike [1], who recently established that every rectifiable or locally monotone Jordan curve inscribes every rectangle. Their work was inspired by the techniques of Greene and Lobb [8], but used microlocal sheaf theory in place of Lagrangian Floer homology.

###### Theorem 1.5 (Asano-Ike, 2026 [1]).

Let γ \gamma be a rectifiable or locally monotone Jordan curve, then γ \gamma inscribes every rectangle.

However, their approach relies on the associated Lagrangians being monotone, a property that fails to hold in the setting of isosceles trapezoids. It is therefore not clear how their methods could be adapted to address the problem of inscribing isosceles trapezoids.

### 1.1 Why isosceles trapezoids?

To motivate our interest in inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids, as opposed to any other class of quadrilaterals, we consider the following result for cyclic quadrilaterals.

###### Theorem 1.6 (Greene-Lobb, 2020 [7]).

Every smooth Jordan curve inscribes every cyclic quadrilateral.

This theorem is optimal in two ways: any quadrilateral that is not cyclic does not inscribe in a circle (which is a smooth Jordan curve); and for any cyclic quadrilateral that is not an isosceles trapezoid there exists a non-smooth Jordan curve (in fact a triangle) in which it does not inscribe. This both motivates our interest in isosceles trapezoids, as the widest class of quadrilaterals for which the problem of inscription on general Jordan curves remains open, and demonstrates an obstruction to progress on the square peg problem: the issue of *shrinkout*.

#### 1.1.1 The issue of shrinkout

Suppose that γ \gamma is a continuous Jordan curve in ℂ \mathbb{C}. The Riemann mapping theorem tells us that there exists a biholomorphic map f: 𝔻 → D f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow D, where 𝔻 = { z ∈ ℂ: | z | < 1 } \mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\} and D D is the bounded region of ℂ ∖ γ \mathbb{C}\setminus\gamma. Carathéodory’s extension to the Riemann mapping theorem tells us that this map continuously extends to a homeomorphism f: 𝔻 ¯ → D ¯ f:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow\overline{D}. The Jordan curve γ \gamma is therefore the limit of a sequence of smooth Jordan curves γ n \gamma_{n}, the images of concentric circles in 𝔻 \mathbb{D} approaching the unit circle ∂ 𝔻 \partial\mathbb{D} from the interior.

Fix a cyclic quadrilateral Q Q. From Theorem 1.6, we know that each smooth Jordan curve γ n \gamma_{n} inscribes any given cyclic quadrilateral, so we get a sequence of inscriptions Q n ⊂ γ n Q_{n}\subset\gamma_{n} where Q n Q_{n} is an inscription of Q Q in γ n \gamma_{n}. Compactness ensures that there exists a convergent subsequence Q n i Q_{n_{i}} of inscriptions of Q Q. It is therefore tempting to conclude that γ \gamma must inscribe Q Q. However, in the limit the inscription of Q Q may shrink to a single point. From the discussion under Theorem 1.6, we know that this is necessarily the case when Q Q is a cyclic quadrilateral that is not an isosceles trapezoid and the limiting curve γ \gamma is a triangle that does not inscribe Q Q. We call this phenomenon *shrinkout*.

To approach the issue of shrinkout, we use the same approach as [8, 9], that is, we use a version of Lagrangian Floer homology called Jordan Floer homology, which has an associated spectral invariant. We will show that this spectral invariant relates to an area associated with the inscribed isosceles trapezoids, so controlling the variation of this spectral invariant can be used in special cases to obstruct shrinkout.

Our initial aim is to show that non-trivial intersection points exist, for which we use Lagrangian Floer homology.

### 1.3 Jordan Floer homology

The approach to construct Jordan Floer homology associated with inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids will be broadly the same as that used by Greene and Lobb for the case of rectangles in [8], now with an extra parameter r r. Together, the aspect ratio r r and angle θ \theta parametrise the space of isosceles trapezoids up to similarity (as described above) with the case r = 1 / 2 r=1/2 representing rectangles. We now provide an outline of the approach, omitting technical details.

Given a Jordan curve γ ⊂ ℂ \gamma\subset\mathbb{C}, we consider the torus γ × γ ⊂ ℂ 2 \gamma\times\gamma\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}. This torus is Lagrangian in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} with respect to the symplectic form ω = ( 1 − r) ⋅ d ​ x 1 ∧ d ​ y 1 + r ⋅ d ​ x 2 ∧ d ​ y 2 \omega=(1-r)\cdot dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}+r\cdot dx_{2}\wedge dy_{2}. We then define a Hamiltonian H H such that the time-1 flow of the associated Hamiltonian vector field X H X_{H} is the transformation G r, θ G_{r,\theta}. This gives a second Lagrangian torus G r, θ ​ ( γ × γ) G_{r,\theta}(\gamma\times\gamma). Generically, these Lagrangian tori intersect cleanly along the loop Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma) and transversally at points of intersection away from this loop, which we call non-trivial intersection points and represent inscribed isosceles trapezoids. To each of these intersection points p p, there is an associated *trajectory*τ: [0, 1] → ℂ 2 \tau:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2} of the Hamiltonian vector field X H X_{H} with τ ⁡ ( 0) ∈ γ × γ \tau(0)\in\gamma\times\gamma and τ ⁡ ( 1) = p \tau(1)=p. Non-trivial intersection points are in one-to-one correspondence with non-constant trajectories.

We then construct a chain complex, the Jordan Floer chain complex JFC ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \operatorname{JFC}(\gamma,r,\theta), generically generated by the non-constant trajectories of H H. The differential ∂ \partial on this chain complex is defined as a count of certain strips between trajectories, called *pseudo-holomorphic strips*. We only consider pseudo-holomorphic strips that avoid the diagonal Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) = { ( z, z) ∣ z ∈ ℂ } \Delta(\mathbb{C})=\{(z,z)\mid z\in\mathbb{C}\}. Showing that this differential satisfies ∂ 2 = 0 \partial^{2}=0 is one of the key technical steps in defining Jordan Floer homology, as it requires proving that the limits of diagonal-avoiding pseudo-holomorphic strips stay away from the diagonal. Fortunately, some of the arguments for establishing Jordan Floer homology for inscriptions of rectangles made in [8] extend directly to the isosceles trapezoid case. Taking the homology of the Jordan Floer chain complex gives the Jordan Floer homology JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta). Since generators of the chain complex correspond to inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids of aspect ratio r r and angle θ \theta, showing that JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta) is non-trivial is sufficient to show that γ \gamma inscribes such an isosceles trapezoid.

The Jordan Floer chain complex has a real filtration called the action, which lifts to a filtration grading for each non-zero homology class α ∈ JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \alpha\in\operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta) called the spectral invariant of α \alpha. There is a single non-zero homology class in JF 2 ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \operatorname{JF}_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta), whose associated spectral invariant we denote by l 2 ​ ( γ, r, θ) l_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta). This spectral invariant can be used to obstruct *shrinkout*when considering non-smooth Jordan curve limits (see Section 1.1.1).

### 1.4 The main result

The main goal of this paper is to construct and examine Jordan Floer homology for inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in Jordan curves. Two classes of curves of interest are rectifiable Jordan curves, which are those of finite length, and locally monotone Jordan curves, which we now define.

## 2 Jordan Floer homology

The goal of this section is to construct the Jordan Floer homology for the following data: a smooth Jordan curve γ ⊂ ℂ \gamma\subset\mathbb{C}, an aspect ratio r ∈ ( 0, 1 / 2] r\in(0,1/2], an angle θ ∈ ( 0, π) \theta\in(0,\pi), a Hamiltonian perturbation h t: ℂ 2 → ℂ 2 h_{t}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}, and an almost complex structure J t: T ​ ℂ 2 → T ​ ℂ 2 J_{t}:T\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow T\mathbb{C}^{2}. This will closely follow the construction in [8], which constructs the Jordan Floer homology associated with inscriptions of rectangles in smooth Jordan curves.

The first step is to define the Jordan Floer complex JFC ⁡ ( γ, r, θ, h t) \operatorname{JFC}(\gamma,r,\theta,h_{t}) and its differential ∂ ( γ, r, θ, h t, J t) \partial_{(\gamma,r,\theta,h_{t},J_{t})}. The first main result will be verifying that this map squares to zero. This requires verifying that limits of sequences of pseudo-holomorphic strips that avoid the diagonal behave nicely, that is, they do not admit bubbling and any breaking occurs away from the diagonal. This is the main technical step in defining the Jordan Floer homology for the given data.

We then consider chain maps

 | JFC ⁡ ( γ, r, θ 1, h t 1, J t 1) ⟶ JFC ⁡ ( γ, r, θ 2, h t 2, J t 2), \operatorname{JFC}(\gamma,r,\theta_{1},h_{t}^{1},J_{t}^{1})\longrightarrow\operatorname{JFC}(\gamma,r,\theta_{2},h_{t}^{2},J_{t}^{2}), |  |

known as continuation maps. They provide isomorphisms on the associated homology groups, demonstrating that the Jordan Floer homology groups JF ⁡ ( γ, s, θ, h t, J t) \operatorname{JF}(\gamma,s,\theta,h_{t},J_{t}) are independent of the Hamiltonian perturbation h t h_{t} and the almost-complex structure J t J_{t}. The homology groups can therefore be written simply as JF ⁡ ( γ, s, θ) \operatorname{JF}(\gamma,s,\theta) and we have the isomorphisms induced from the continuation maps

 | JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ 1) ⟶ JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ 2). \operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta_{1})\longrightarrow\operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta_{2}). |  |

Careful treatment of the limit as θ → 0 \theta\rightarrow 0 then allows us to determine the Jordan Floer homology for any aspect ratio r r and angle θ \theta up to isomorphism as the Morse homology of a torus γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma relative to its diagonal curve Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma).

### 2.2 Jordan Floer chain complex

One issue we wish to avoid is that the Lagrangians may not intersect transversely away from the diagonal. To achieve transversality, we introduce a small Hamiltonian perturbation. In a small neighbourhood of the diagonal Δ ⁡ ( γ) = { ( z, z) ∣ z ∈ γ } \Delta(\gamma)=\{(z,z)\mid z\in\gamma\}, we can ensure that γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi^{1}_{H_{t}}(\gamma\times\gamma) only intersect at the diagonal, and hence we do not require a Hamiltonian perturbation in this region. This will be an important technical detail later.

###### Definition 2.6 (Width).

The *width*of a smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma, denoted Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) \operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma) is the infimal diagonal length of an inscribed isosceles trapezoid with fixed aspect ratio r ∈ ( 0, 1 / 2] r\in(0,1/2] and angle θ ∈ ( 0, π) \theta\in(0,\pi).

Note that we cannot define the width of a smooth Jordan curve as the infimal diagonal length of an inscribed isosceles trapezoid (with no restriction on r r or θ \theta), as would be analogous to the definition used in [8] for the Jordan Floer complex associated with rectangles, since for any fixed r ≠ 1 / 2 r\neq 1/2 and for any smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma it is possible to find a sequence of inscribed isosceles trapezoids that degenerate to a point as θ → π \theta\rightarrow\pi. With our slightly modified definition of width, we now recall some key definitions from [8] in order to set consistent notation and terminology.

###### Definition 2.7 (Admissible Hamiltonian perturbation).

Fix a smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma, an aspect ratio 0 < r ≤ 1 / 2 0<r\leq 1/2, and an angle 0 < θ < π 0<\theta<\pi. A time dependent Hamiltonian perturbation h t: ℂ 2 → ℝ h_{t}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1 0\leq t\leq 1 is called *admissible*if it satisfies:

1. 1.

h t = 0 h_{t}=0 whenever 0 ≤ t ≤ 0.1 0\leq t\leq 0.1 or 0.9 ≤ t ≤ 1 0.9\leq t\leq 1,

2. 2.

h t ​ ( z, w) = 0 h_{t}(z,w)=0 whenever | z − w | ≤ Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) / 2 |z-w|\leq\operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma)/2,

3. 3.

the Lagrangians γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t + h t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi_{H_{t}+h_{t}}^{1}(\gamma\times\gamma) intersect cleanly along Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma) and transversely elsewhere.

Moreover, we call ( γ, r, θ, h t) (\gamma,r,\theta,h_{t}) an *admissible quadruple*.

Condition (1) ensures that H t + h t = 0 H_{t}+h_{t}=0 whenever t ∈ [0, 0.1] ∪ [0.9, 1] t\in[0,0.1]\cup[0.9,1], which means that the Cauchy-Riemann-Floer equation reduces to the Cauchy-Riemann equation for such values of t t. Condition (2) ensures that in a neighbourhood of Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma) we have Φ H t + h t 1 = Φ H t 1 = G r, θ \Phi_{H_{t}+h_{t}}^{1}=\Phi_{H_{t}}^{1}=G_{r,\theta}, which means that the condition Lagrangians γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t + h t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi_{H_{t}+h_{t}}^{1}(\gamma\times\gamma) intersect cleanly along Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma) is automatically satisfied. Since all other intersections of γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi_{H_{t}}^{1}(\gamma\times\gamma) occur at a distance of at least Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) \operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma) from the diagonal, condition (3) reduces to the standard transversality requirement in Lagrangian Floer theory that ensures nice behaviour of the moduli spaces.

By the above discussion, a standard transversality argument on the space of Hamiltonians satisfying conditions (1) and (2) establishes the following lemma.

###### Lemma 2.8.

The set of admissible Hamiltonian perturbations is a dense open subset of the set of time dependent Hamiltonians h t: ℂ 2 → ℝ h_{t}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} satisfying conditions (1) and (2) of admissible Hamiltonian perturbations with respect to the C ∞ C^{\infty} topology. ∎

Recall that we denote by X H X_{H} the Hamiltonian vector field associated with the Hamiltonian H H, uniquely determined by i X H ​ ω ≔ ω ⁡ ( X H, ⋅) = d ​ H i_{X_{H}}\omega\coloneqq\omega(X_{H},\cdot)=dH.

###### Definition 2.9 (Trajectory).

A *trajectory*τ: [0, 1] → ℂ 2 \tau:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2} of a Hamiltonian H H is an integral curve of the vector field X H X_{H}, that is τ ′ ​ ( t) = X H ∘ τ ⁡ ( t) \tau^{\prime}(t)=X_{H}\circ\tau(t), with τ ⁡ ( 0), τ ⁡ ( 1) ∈ γ × γ \tau(0),\tau(1)\in\gamma\times\gamma.

Trajectories are flow lines of the vector field, so we have τ ⁡ ( 1) = Φ H 1 ​ ( τ ⁡ ( 0)) \tau(1)=\Phi_{H}^{1}(\tau(0)) and hence the intersection points of γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t + h t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi_{H_{t}+h_{t}}^{1}(\gamma\times\gamma) are in one-to-one correspondence with the trajectories of H t + h t H_{t}+h_{t}. Moreover, the constant trajectories are exactly those which lie in the diagonal and the non-constant trajectories are disjoint from the diagonal, meaning the non-trivial intersections are in one-to-one correspondence with non-constant trajectories.

An important observation is that any non-constant trajectory of H t + h t H_{t}+h_{t} is not only disjoint from Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) \Delta(\mathbb{C}), but remains a distance at least Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) / 2 \operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma)/2 away from Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) \Delta(\mathbb{C}). This is presented as Lemma 2.7 in [8], but we outline the argument here. Let N N be an open neighbourhood of the diagonal Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) \Delta(\mathbb{C}) defined by N = { ( z, w): | z − w | ≤ Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) / 2 } N=\{(z,w):|z-w|\leq\operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma)/2\}. By the definition of an admissible Hamiltonian perturbation h t h_{t}, within the region N N we have H t + h t = H t H_{t}+h_{t}=H_{t}, but any trajectory of H t H_{t} (by the definition of H t H_{t}) stays a fixed distance from Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) \Delta(\mathbb{C}). Therefore, any trajectory must remain fully inside or outside N N. If a trajectory τ \tau lies entirely within N N, then it must be a trajectory of H t H_{t} and therefore τ ⁡ ( 0) = ( z, w) \tau(0)=(z,w) consists of the endpoints of an inscribes isosceles trapezoid of aspect ratio r r and angle θ \theta, contradicting the definition of Width r, θ ⁡ ( γ) \operatorname{Width}_{r,\theta}(\gamma).

In order to define a Lagrangian Floer chain complex, one typically requires the notion of cappings of trajectories. These are of particular importance when defining the differential, the symplectic action, and the Maslov index.

Typically, the generators of the Lagrangian Floer chain complex are then simply pairs ( τ, [τ ^]) (\tau,[\widehat{\tau}]) where τ \tau is a trajectory. There are two reasons why we take a slightly different approach.

The first is that, as discussed above, the non-constant trajectories of H t + h t H_{t}+h_{t} are in one-to-one correspondence with the non-trivial intersections of γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma and Φ H t + h t 1 ​ ( γ × γ) \Phi_{H_{t}+h_{t}}^{1}(\gamma\times\gamma). We therefore only consider generators ( τ, [τ ^]) (\tau,[\widehat{\tau}]) where τ \tau is a non-constant trajectory.

The second is that in the Jordan Floer setting, there is a canonical choice of capping up to homotopy for non-constant trajectories that we call the *preferred capping*.

###### Definition 2.11 (Preferred Capping).

For any non-constant trajectory τ \tau, a capping τ ^ \widehat{\tau} is called a *preferred capping*of τ \tau if it is disjoint from the diagonal, that is, im ⁡ ( τ ^) ∩ Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) = ∅ \operatorname{im}(\widehat{\tau})\cap\Delta(\mathbb{C})=\emptyset.

###### Lemma 2.12.

Let τ \tau be a non-constant trajectory, then all preferred cappings τ ^ \widehat{\tau} belong to the same homotopy class of capping [τ ^] [\widehat{\tau}].

###### Proof.

Recall that non-constant trajectories are disjoint from the diagonal. We note that the homotopy class of a preferred capping τ ^ \widehat{\tau} is completely determined by the homotopy class of τ ^ ​ ( 1, ⋅): [0, 1] → ( γ × γ) ∖ Δ ⁡ ( γ) \widehat{\tau}(1,\cdot):[0,1]\rightarrow(\gamma\times\gamma)\setminus\Delta(\mathbb{\gamma}) in ( γ × γ) ∖ Δ ⁡ ( γ) (\gamma\times\gamma)\setminus\Delta(\mathbb{\gamma}) relative to its endpoints. The result is then immediate, as the boundary of a preferred capping must have winding number 0 0 around Δ ⁡ ( ℂ) \Delta(\mathbb{C}). ∎

### 2.3 Pseudo-holomorphic strips

To define the Floer differential, we must first define *pseudo-holomorphic strips*, which satisfy a Cauchy-Riemann-Floer equation, and then define *moduli spaces*of these pseudo-holomorphic strips. Given an admissible quadruple ( γ, r, θ, h t) (\gamma,r,\theta,h_{t}), we must therefore choose a smooth almost-complex structure J J on ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} compatible with ω \omega. More precisely, we wish to choose a smoothly varying time-dependent almost-complex structure J t J_{t} that ensures that the moduli spaces of pseudo-holomorphic strips are *nice*in the sense that they consist of manifolds of the expected dimension.

### 2.4 Jordan Floer differential

We now define the differential on our chain complex, which is defined in terms of counts of connected components of the 1-dimensional moduli spaces. We introduce the notation ℳ i \mathcal{M}_{i} to denote the union of all the i i -dimensional components of the moduli space ℳ \mathcal{M}.

### 2.5 The continuation package

Currently we require a choice of Hamiltonian perturbation and almost-complex structure in order to define the Jordan Floer homology. In Lagrangian Floer homology, there is a standard procedure for showing that the homology is independent of these choices, which requires chain maps known as *continuation maps*that induce isomorphisms on homology. Similarly to the differential, continuation maps are defined by a count of pseudo-holomorphic strips. In the Jordan Floer setting, we can improve on this. For a given aspect ratio r r, we can show that the Jordan Floer homology is independent of not only the choice of Hamiltonian perturbation h t h_{t} and almost-complex structure J t J_{t}, but also of the angle θ \theta. A detailed treatment of continuation maps in the more general setting is given in [2, Chapter 11].

#### 2.5.3 Varying the aspect ratio

Since Jordan Floer homology is independent (up to isomorphism) of the aspect ratio r r and the angle θ \theta, it is natural to hope for a continuation package for varying r r. It is therefore unclear how to define continuation maps as counts of pseudo-holomorphic strips, as to define pseudo-holomorphic strips we first must make a choice of symplectic form.

## 3 The symplectic action and spectral invariants

The Jordan Floer homology has an integer homological grading, supported in degrees 1 and 2, called the Maslov index. The Jordan Floer chain complex also has a real filtration called the action. This lifts to a filtration grading for each non-zero homology class α ∈ JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \alpha\in\operatorname{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta), called the spectral invariant of α \alpha. Recalling that Jordan Floer homology has a single homology class in each of the degrees it is supported, we obtain two spectral invariants l 1 ​ ( γ, r, θ) l_{1}(\gamma,r,\theta) and l 2 ​ ( γ, r, θ) l_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta). We can use these spectral invariants to obstruct *shrinkout*(see Section 1.1.1).

To introduce the symplectic action, we first introduce the space on which it is defined. We define the path space Ω ⁡ ( ℂ 2, γ × γ) \Omega(\mathbb{C}^{2},\gamma\times\gamma) as the set of paths σ: [0, 1] → ℂ 2 \sigma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2} with endpoints in γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma. Unfortunately, the notion of a preferred capping (see Definition 2.11) cannot be extended to all such paths. To deal with this problem, it is natural to introduce the diagonal avoiding path space

 | Ω ~ ​ ( ℂ 2, γ × γ) = Ω ⁡ ( ℂ 2 ∖ Δ ⁡ ( ℂ), ( γ × γ) ∖ Δ ⁡ ( γ)) \widetilde{\Omega}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\gamma\times\gamma)=\Omega\bigl(\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\Delta(\mathbb{C}),(\gamma\times\gamma)\setminus\Delta(\gamma)\bigr) |  |

as the diagonal avoiding subset of Ω ⁡ ( ℂ 2, γ × γ) \Omega(\mathbb{C}^{2},\gamma\times\gamma). The key observation is that the definition of a preferred capping can be extended to any diagonal avoiding path, and therefore we can define the symplectic action on this space.

###### Definition 3.1 (Symplectic action).

For a path σ ∈ Ω ~ ​ ( ℂ 2, γ × γ) \sigma\in\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\gamma\times\gamma) we define its action with respect to the Hamiltonian H t H_{t} by

where σ ^ \widehat{\sigma} is a *preferred capping*of σ \sigma.

The action functional 𝒜 H t \mathcal{A}_{H_{t}} plays the role of a Morse function on the path space Ω ~ ​ ( ℂ 2, γ × γ) \widetilde{\Omega}(\mathbb{C}^{2},\gamma\times\gamma). Its critical points correspond precisely to the non-constant trajectories of the Hamiltonian vector field X H t X_{H_{t}}. In fact, an alternative approach to Floer homology is to attempt to apply Morse theory on the path space, using the action functional as the analogue of a Morse function. This approach is outlined by Oh in [15, Chapter 12] and requires the Hamiltonian to be *regular*, similarly to how we required the Hamiltonian to be *admissible*to ensure transverse intersections of the Lagrangians.

Assuming H t H_{t} is regular, the Floer differential lowers the action, filtering the Floer chain complex. The spectral invariants are then defined as the filtration grading of a given homology class in the induced filtration on the Floer homology. In the case where H t H_{t} is not regular, spectral invariants can still be obtained by considering limits of sequences of spectral invariants associated with regular Hamiltonians H t n H_{t}^{n} limiting to H t H_{t}. Moreover, such spectral invariants still represent actions of trajectories of the Hamiltonian vector field, however, these trajectories may not correspond to transverse intersections of the Lagrangians. A detailed introduction to spectral invariants is provided in [11].

#### 3.1.3 Action limit for large angles

We now return to considering the limits of sequences of inscribed isosceles trapezoids T θ n T_{\theta_{n}} in a given Jordan curve γ \gamma and the associated actions 𝒜 r, θ n ​ ( T θ n) \mathcal{A}_{r,\theta_{n}}(T_{\theta_{n}}) as θ n → π \theta_{n}\rightarrow\pi. For r = 1 / 2 r=1/2, the case of rectangles, such sequences of inscribed rectangles in a smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma always limit to binormals, whose action equals half the area of the region bounded by the Jordan curve. For r ∈ ( 0, 1 / 2) r\in(0,1/2), there are two distinct possibilities for the limit.

##### Shrinkout

The first possibility is that the isosceles trapezoids shrink to a point in the limit, that is, T θ n → p ∈ γ T_{\theta_{n}}\rightarrow p\in\gamma. Unlike the case of shrinkout when varying the Jordan curve, this variety of shrinkout does not pose any problems. In fact, this is the simpler case to deal with, largely due to the following result.

##### Quadrisecants

The second possibility is that the isosceles trapezoids limit to a *quadrisecant*, that is, the vertices of the isosceles trapezoid limit to four different points that lie on a straight line. Each quadrisecant Q ∈ T r, π Q\in T_{r,\pi} is the limit of two disjoint one parameter families of inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids T θ i T^{i}_{\theta} parametrised by θ ∈ ( π − ε, π) \theta\in(\pi-\varepsilon,\pi) for ε \varepsilon sufficiently small. An example is given in Figure 7. Therefore, each quadrisecant has two associated actions given by the limits of the actions of the inscribed isosceles trapezoids T θ i T^{i}_{\theta} as θ → π \theta\rightarrow\pi. An interesting result is that the sum of the two actions associated with any given quadrisecant is simply the area of the Jordan curve.

Unfortunately, despite the duality result above, the action in the quadrisecant case is significantly more difficult to analyse than in the shrinkout case. In particular, an action associated with a quadrisecant can be made arbitrarily large or small simply by varying the Jordan curve, whilst keeping the area of the Jordan curve constant and keeping the points at which the quadrisecant is inscribed fixed. It is this lack of control over the action in the limit as θ → π \theta\rightarrow\pi that makes the isosceles trapezoid case more difficult to approach than the rectangular case.

### 3.2 Spectral invariants

We recall that the action functional can be viewed as the analogue of the Morse function in Morse theory. In Morse theory, the flow lines counted in defining the differential always point in the direction of decreasing function value. Similarly, the Jordan Floer differential ∂ JF \partial_{\mathrm{JF}} decreases the action. Consequently, Jordan Floer homology JF ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \mathrm{JF}(\gamma,r,\theta) is filtered by the action. We can then define the spectral invariant l α ​ ( γ, r, θ) l_{\alpha}(\gamma,r,\theta) of a homology class α \alpha as the lowest filtration grading in which a representative of α \alpha is supported.

#### 3.2.2 Triangle inequality for the spectral invariants

Another important property of spectral invariants is that they satisfy a triangle inequality, which can be found in [11] and was previously used in [10] in the rectangle setting. In our setting, the triangle equality of relevance is purely for the spectral invariant in homological degree 2 and it plays an important role in the obstruction of shrinkout.

###### Lemma 3.10.

Let γ \gamma be a smooth Jordan curve, r ∈ ( 0, 1 / 2] r\in(0,1/2] an aspect ratio, and θ 1, θ 2 ∈ [0, π] \theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi] angles such that θ 1 + θ 2 ≤ π \theta_{1}+\theta_{2}\leq\pi, then we have the triangle inequality for the homological degree 2 spectral invariant

 | l 2 ​ ( γ, r, θ 1 + θ 2) ≤ l 2 ​ ( γ, r, θ 1) + l 2 ​ ( γ, r, θ 2). l_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\leq l_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta_{1})+l_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta_{2}). |  |

###### Proof.

This follows immediately from the triangle inequality in [11, Theorem 27] once we establish that the product on Floer homology ⋆: HF k ⁡ ( L, H 1, J 1) ⊗ HF l ⁡ ( L, H 2, J 2) → HF k + l − n ⁡ ( L, H, J) \star:\operatorname{HF}_{k}(L;H_{1},J_{1})\otimes\operatorname{HF}_{l}(L;H_{2},J_{2})\rightarrow\operatorname{HF}_{k+l-n}(L;H,J) defined in [19, Section 3.9.1] satisfies α ⋆ α = α \alpha\star\alpha=\alpha in the Jordan Floer homology setting where α ∈ JF 2 ⁡ ( γ, r, θ) \alpha\in\operatorname{JF}_{2}(\gamma,r,\theta) is the generator of the second homology group. This product respects continuation maps. Therefore, it is sufficient to establish the result in the Morse-Bott homology limit as θ → 0 \theta\rightarrow 0. We recall that this limit is simply the relative homology of a torus γ × γ \gamma\times\gamma to its diagonal curve Δ ⁡ ( γ) \Delta(\gamma), for which α \alpha is the relative fundamental class and therefore acts as a unit, giving the required result. ∎

## 4 Inscriptions in non-smooth Jordan curves

We now wish to consider inscriptions of isosceles trapezoids in non-smooth Jordan curves. As first introduced in the discussion of the issue of shrinkout (Section 1.1.1), the approach will be to approximate a non-smooth Jordan curve γ \gamma by a sequence of smooth Jordan curves γ n \gamma_{n}. Convergent sequences of inscribed isosceles trapezoids T n T_{n} in the smooth Jordan curves γ n \gamma_{n} limit to some set of points T ⊂ γ T\subset\gamma. However, the limiting set T T could be a single point, that is, the inscriptions T n T_{n}*shrinkout*. The aim is therefore to obstruct shrinkout.

We will restrict ourselves to considering *rectifiable*and *locally monotone*Jordan curves. Recall that rectifiable Jordan curves are those of finite length, and locally monotone Jordan curves can be represented locally as the graph of a continuous function. Such Jordan curves are easier to deal with since they can be approximated by smooth Jordan curves with nice properties, making it easier to obstruct shrinkout.

### 4.1 Approximating Curves

We introduced the standard approach to approximating non-smooth Jordan curves in Section 1.1.1. This is the approach we will use when approximating rectifiable Jordan curves. Let γ ⊂ ℂ \gamma\subset\mathbb{C} be a Jordan curve and D D be the bounded region of ℂ ∖ γ \mathbb{C}\setminus\gamma. By the Riemann mapping theorem, there exists a biholomorphic map f: 𝔻 → D f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow D, where 𝔻 ⊂ ℂ \mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{C} is the open unit disc. By Carathéodory’s extension to the Riemann mapping theorem, this extends continuously to a homeomorphism f: 𝔻 ¯ → D ¯ f:\overline{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow\overline{D}.

We can then define smooth Jordan curves γ t = f ⁡ ( C t) \gamma_{t}=f(C_{t}), where C t = { z ∈ ℂ ∣ | z | = t } C_{t}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z|=t\} is the circle centred at the origin of radius t t. These Jordan curves have a natural parametrisation given by γ t ​ ( θ) = f ⁡ ( t ​ e i ​ θ) \gamma_{t}(\theta)=f(te^{i\theta}). An important property of such approximating Jordan curves is given by the following classical theorem.

Let γ \gamma be a rectifiable Jordan curve of length L L and let γ t \gamma_{t} be defined as above, then γ t \gamma_{t} are of uniformly bounded length.
