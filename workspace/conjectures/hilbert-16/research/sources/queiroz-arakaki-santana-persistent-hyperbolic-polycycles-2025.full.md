## Abstract

In this work we consider families of smooth vector fields having a persistent polycycle with *n*hyperbolic saddles. We derive the asymptotic expansion of the return map associated to the polycycle, determining its leading terms. As a consequence, we obtain conditions on the leading terms that allow us to determine the cyclicity of such polycycles. A general sufficient condition for the stability of polycycles with graphic number equal to one is also obtained. We then apply our results to study the cyclicity of a polycycle of a model with applications in Game Theory.

### A Generalized Binomial Theorem

Given \(\alpha \in {\mathbb {C}}\) and \(k\in {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\), the *generalized binomial coefficient*is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \left( {\begin{array}{c}\alpha \\ k\end{array}}\right) =\frac{\alpha (\alpha -1)\dots (\alpha -k+1)}{k!}, \end{aligned}$$

(42)

with the convention \(\left( {\begin{array}{c}\alpha \\ 0\end{array}}\right) =1\). Observe that if \(\alpha \in {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant k}\), then ( [42][82]) reduces to the usual binomial coefficient.

### Theorem 3

(Generalized Binomial Theorem) Let *x*, *y*, \(\alpha \in {\mathbb {C}}\) such that \(|x|>|y|\). Then

$$\begin{aligned} (x+y)^\alpha =\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }\left( {\begin{array}{c}\alpha \\ k\end{array}}\right) x^{\alpha -k}y^k. \end{aligned}$$

### Proof

Since \(x\ne 0\) it follows that \(t=y/x\in {\mathbb {C}}\) is well defined and thus we can consider the holomorphic function \(f:{\mathbb {C}}\rightarrow {\mathbb {C}}\) given by,

$$\begin{aligned} f(t)=(1+t)^\alpha . \end{aligned}$$

From \(f^{(k)}(t)=\alpha (\alpha -1)\dots (\alpha -k+1)(1+t)^{\alpha -k}\) it follows that expanding *f*in Maclaurin’s series we get

$$\begin{aligned} (1+t)^\alpha =\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}t^k=\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }\frac{\alpha (\alpha -1)\dots (\alpha -k+1)}{k!}t^k=\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }\left( {\begin{array}{c}\alpha \\ k\end{array}}\right) t^k, \end{aligned}$$

with the series converging provided \(|t|<1\). The theorem now follows by replacing \(t=y/x\) and multiplying the equation by \(x^\alpha \). \(\square \)

### B Coefficient expressions for the Dulac map

In this section, we present the expressions for the coefficients \(\Delta _{00}, \Delta _{10}\) and \(\Delta _{01}\) obtained by Marín and Villadelprat in [[16][83], Theorem A], for the case in which the unstable and stable separatrices are contained the *x*-axis and *y*-axis, respectively (recall Figure [2][84]). Considering the vector field ( [10][85]), we define the following functions:

$$\begin{aligned}&L_1(u):=\exp \int _{0}^{u}\biggl (\frac{P(0,y;\mu )}{Q(0,y;\mu )}+\frac{1}{\lambda }\biggr )\frac{dy}{y},&\quad&L_2(u):=\exp \int _{0}^{u}\biggl (\frac{Q(x,0;\mu )}{P(x,0;\mu )}+\lambda \biggr )\frac{dx}{x},\nonumber \\&M_1(u):=L_1(u)\partial _x\left( \frac{P}{Q}\right) (0,u),&\quad&M_2(u):=L_2(u)\partial _y\left( \frac{Q}{P}\right) (u,0), \end{aligned}$$

(43)

Let \(\sigma _{ijk}\) denote the *k*th derivative at \(s=0\) of the *j*th component of the transverse section \(\sigma _i=(\sigma _{i,1},\sigma _{i,2})\), more precisely,

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma _{ijk}=\partial _s^k\sigma _{i,j}(0;\mu ). \end{aligned}$$

Now, we define the following quantities:

$$\begin{aligned}&S_1:=\frac{\sigma _{112}}{2\sigma _{111}}-\frac{\sigma _{121}}{\sigma _{120}}\left( \frac{P}{Q}\right) (0,\sigma _{120})-\frac{\sigma _{111}}{L_1(\sigma _{120})}{\hat{M}}_1(1/\lambda ,\sigma _{120}),\nonumber \\&S_2:=\frac{\sigma _{222}}{2\sigma _{221}}-\frac{\sigma _{211}}{\sigma _{210}}\left( \frac{Q}{P}\right) (\sigma _{210},0)-\frac{\sigma _{221}}{L_2(\sigma _{210})}{\hat{M}}_2(\lambda ,\sigma _{210}), \end{aligned}$$

(44)

where \({\hat{M}}_i\) denotes a sort of incomplete Melin transform. We refer the reader to [[16][83], Appendix B] for a detailed study. For our purposes, the following result suffices to perform accurate computations.

### Proposition 4

([[16][83], Theorem B.1]) Consider an open interval \(I\subset {\mathbb {R}}\) containing \(x=0\) and an open subset \(U\subset {\mathbb {R}}^N\).

1. (a)

Given \(f(x;\nu )\in C^\infty (I\times U)\), there exists a unique \({\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )\in C^\infty (({\mathbb {R}}\setminus {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant 0})\times I\times U)\) such that

$$\begin{aligned} x\partial _x{\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )-\alpha {\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )=f(x;\nu ); \end{aligned}$$

2. (b)

If \(x\in I\setminus \{0\}\), then \(\partial _x({\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )|x|^{-\alpha })=f(x;\nu )\frac{|x|^{-\alpha }}{x}\) and, taking any \(k\in {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\), with \(k>\alpha \),

$$\begin{aligned} {\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )=\sum _{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\partial _x^if(0;\nu )}{i! (i-\alpha )}x^i+|x|^\alpha \int _{0}^{x}\biggl (f(s;\nu )-T^{k-1}_0f(s;\nu )\biggr )|s|^{-\alpha }\frac{ds}{s}, \end{aligned}$$

where \(T^k_0f(x;\nu )=\sum _{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{i!}\partial _x^if(0;\nu )x^i\) is the *k*th degree Taylor polynomial of \(f(x;\nu )\) at \(x=0\);

3. (c)

For each \((i_0,x_0;\nu _0)\in {\mathbb {Z}}\times I\times U\) the function \((\alpha ,x;\nu )\mapsto (i_0-\alpha ){\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )\) extends \(C^\infty \) at \((i_0,x_0;\nu _0)\) and, moreover, it tends to \(\frac{1}{i_0!}\partial _x^{i_0}f(0;\nu _0)x_0^{i_0}\) as \((\alpha ,x;\nu )\rightarrow (i_0,x_0;\nu _0)\);

4. (d)

If \(f(x;\nu )\) is analytic on \(I\times U\), then \({\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )\) is analytic on \(({\mathbb {R}}\setminus {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant 0})\times I\times U\). Finally, for each \((\alpha _0,x_0;\nu _0)\in {\mathbb {Z}}_{\geqslant 0}\times I \times U\), the function \((\alpha ,x;\nu )\mapsto (\alpha _0-\alpha ){\hat{f}}(\alpha ,x;\nu )\) extends analytically to \((\alpha _0,x_0;\nu _0)\).

The coefficients of the Dulac map are given by the next result.

### Proposition 5

( [[16][83], Theorem A, item (b)]) The coefficients \(\Delta _{ij}\) for \((i,j)\in \{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}\) of the Dulac map are given by

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _{00}=\frac{\sigma _{111}^\lambda \sigma _{120}}{L_1^\lambda (\sigma _{120})}\frac{L_2(\sigma _{210})}{\sigma _{221}\sigma _{210}^\lambda },\quad \Delta _{01}=-(\Delta _{00})^2S_2,\quad \Delta _{10}=\lambda \Delta _{00}S_1. \end{aligned}$$

(45)

### C An ODE model in game theory

We now briefly present the construction of model ( [40][86]). Let \(\Gamma _1,\Gamma _2\) be two players and \(\{X_1,X_2\}\), \(\{Y_1,Y_2\}\) be the respective *pure strategies*. We denote by \(a_{ij}^*\in {\mathbb {R}}\) the payoff of strategy \(X_i\) against \(Y_j\) and by \(b_{ij}^*\in {\mathbb {R}}\) the payoff of strategy \(Y_i\) against \(X_j\). For each probabilistic vector of dimension two

$$\begin{aligned} x=(x_1,x_2)\in S^2:=\{(x_1,x_2)\in {\mathbb {R}}^2:x_1\geqslant 0,x_2\geqslant 0,x_1+x_2=1\}, \end{aligned}$$

we associate a *mix strategy*given by \(x_1 X_1+x_2 X_2\). Similarly, given \(y\in S^2\), we associate the respective mix strategy \(y_1Y_1+y_2Y_2\). Let

$$\begin{aligned} A^*=\left( \begin{array}{cc} a_{11}^*& a_{12}^*\\ a_{21}^*& a_{22}^*\end{array}\right) ,\quad B^*=\left( \begin{array}{cc} b_{11}^*& b_{12}^*\\ b_{21}^*& b_{22}^*\end{array}\right) , \end{aligned}$$

be the *payoff matrices*. Given \(x,y\in S^2\), the *average payoff*of the mix strategy associated to *x*against the mix strategy associated to *y*is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \langle x, A^*y\rangle =a_{11}^*x_1y_1+a_{12}^*x_1y_2+a_{21}^*x_2y_1+a_{22}^*x_2y_2, \end{aligned}$$

and the average payoff of the mix strategy associated to *y*against the mix strategy associated to *x*is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \langle y, B^*x\rangle =b_{11}^*x_1y_1+b_{12}^*x_1y_2+b_{21}^*x_2y_1+b_{22}^*x_2y_2. \end{aligned}$$

The dynamics between players \(\Gamma _1\) and \(\Gamma _2\) is defined by the system of differential equations,

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{array}{l} \dot{x}_1=x_1\left( \langle e_1,A^*y\rangle -\langle x,A^*y\rangle \right) ,\qquad \dot{y}_1=y_1\left( \langle e_1,B^*x\rangle -\langle y,B^*x\rangle \right) ,\\ \dot{x}_2=x_2\left( \langle e_2,A^*y\rangle -\langle x,A^*y\rangle \right) ,\qquad \dot{y}_2=y_2\left( \langle e_2,B^*x\rangle -\langle y,B^*x\rangle \right) . \end{array} \end{aligned}$$

(46)

Essentially, the weight \(x_i\) of the pure strategy \(X_i\) depends on the difference between the payoffs of the pure strategy and the mix strategy. In other words, the bigger this difference, the more superior strategy \(X_i\) is. Since \(x_1+x_2=y_1+y_2=1\), one can consider only the variables \(x_1,y_1\) to study the dynamics of the game. Thus, ( [46][87]) simplifies to

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{array}{l} \dot{x}= x(x-1)\left( a_{22}^*-a_{12}^*+(a_{12}^*+a_{21}^*-a_{11}^*-a_{22}^*)y\right) ,\\ \dot{y}= y(y-1)\left( b_{22}^*-b_{12}^*+(b_{12}^*+b_{21}^*-b_{11}^*-b_{22}^*)x\right) . \end{array} \end{aligned}$$

The search for more realistic models demanded that the payoffs depended on the weights given to strategies \(X_i\) and \(Y_j\) rather than being constants, i.e. \(a_{ij}^*=a_{ij}^*(x,y)\) and \(b_{ij}^*=b_{ij}^*(x,y)\). Hence, assuming \(a_{ij}^*,b_{ij}^*\) polynomial, the model is generally written as system ( [40][86]).

By the above construction, to investigate the dynamics between the players \(\Gamma _1\) and \(\Gamma _2\), it is sufficient to study system ( [40][86]) in the unit square, i.e. \((x,y)\in [0,1]^2\).
