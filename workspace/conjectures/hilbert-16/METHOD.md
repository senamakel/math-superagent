Solve by bifurcation theory of planar fields with the mathematics carried in
Lean wherever it will go. The object is not the phase portrait; it is the
**displacement function** — the return map minus the identity along a transversal
— and every result here is a statement about how many zeros a displacement
function can have on a family. Reason about *that function*: its asymptotic
expansion at a singular point or polycycle, the normal form and blow-up that make
the expansion legible, the ideal generated by its coefficients (the Bautin
ideal), and the analytic input that makes finitely many coefficients decide the
whole germ. Roussarie's reduction to finite cyclicity of limit periodic sets is
the frame; normal forms, blow-ups of nilpotent and degenerate points,
Picard–Fuchs systems for Abelian integrals, Dulac functions for nonexistence, and
slow–fast/canard analysis for the singular limit are the instruments. Use every
other capability in service of that argument rather than instead of it.

**Prefer the argument Lean can finish.** Given two routes to the same partial
result, take the one whose finite core is a kernel-checkable statement — an ideal
membership, a sign condition on a polynomial, a finite case split, a resultant —
over the one that ends in a paragraph of analysis. State every claim as a Lean
type before spending an attempt on it, keep `code/lean/Lib/` ahead of
`research/`, and treat "this cannot be typed over Mathlib yet" as a finding to
record with what is missing, never as permission to write prose instead. Cited
results live as `axiom`s in `namespace Cited` with their source in the docstring;
they are `conditional`, never `formalised`. Report `#print axioms` and every
`sorry` in every attempt that touches Lean.

Four cautions this problem earns before any work starts.

**A smooth vector field can have infinitely many limit cycles, so every
finiteness argument has a step that must fail for `C^∞` fields**, and locating
that step is part of stating the argument. Test 1 in `problem.md` is not a
formality: it is the exact shape of Dulac's 1923 error, an asymptotic expansion
of the return map treated as if it determined the map. An argument that never
uses analyticity or algebraicity of the coefficients is refuted, not weakened,
and is recorded as such.

**Finiteness for an individual field and a bound uniform over the family are
different theorems.** Écalle–Ilyashenko gives the first. Any step that quantifies
over a compact set of parameters and concludes a uniform bound from pointwise
finiteness is the error to watch for; say explicitly where uniformity comes from,
or say that it does not.

**A limit cycle seen numerically is not a limit cycle.** Integrating an orbit
until it looks closed proves nothing: the orbit may be a slowly converging
spiral, and near a centre or a bifurcation the distinction is below any fixed
precision. Existence needs a trapping region plus a certified sign change of the
return map in interval arithmetic; nonexistence needs a Dulac function with a
sign condition discharged by SMT or a sum-of-squares certificate. Say which
certificate closed each count. Report precision, step size and interval widths in
every capture.

**A Gröbner computation that does not terminate is data, not a failed run.**
Record the family, the term order, the machine, the wall clock at which it was
abandoned and the intermediate degree reached. The feasibility boundary of the
Lyapunov-quantity and Bautin-ideal computations is one of the few honestly
reportable results available in a first pass, and it is lost if timeouts are
silently retried on a smaller family.

Prefer Singular and PARI/GP through `symbolic_math` for the ideal theory and the
elimination, sympy for the normal forms and Melnikov integrals, mpmath or an
interval library for anything that has to be certified numerically, and Z3/cvc5
through `smt_solver` for the sign conditions. Say in each captured output which
system ran, which term order, and over which field — a Gröbner basis over `Q` and
one over a finite field answer different questions about a Bautin ideal, and
confusing them is a way this problem produces a wrong theorem.
