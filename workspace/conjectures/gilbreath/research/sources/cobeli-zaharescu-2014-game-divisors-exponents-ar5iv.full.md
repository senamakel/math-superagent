# A game with divisors and absolute differences of exponents

###### Abstract.

In this work, we discuss a number game that develops in a manner similar to that on which Gilbreath’s conjecture on iterated absolute differences between consecutive primes is formulated. In our case the action occurs at the exponent level and there, the evolution is reminiscent of that in a final Ducci game. We present features of the whole field of the game created by the successive generations, prove an analog of Gilbreath’s conjecture, and raise some open questions.

###### Key words and phrases:

Ducci game, Gilbreath’s conjecture, primes game, absolute differences, Sierpinski triangle.

###### 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification

Primary 11B85; Secondary 39A10.

## 1. Introduction

We study a cellular automaton, whose different facets display characteristics that are both multiplicative and additive in nature. Essentially, it is defined by an ’atomic transformation’ function, which is given by an arithmetical operation. To any integers a a, b b we associate the integer

 | Z ⁡ ( a, b):= a ​ b ( gcd ⁡ ( a, b)) 2. Z(a,b):=\frac{ab}{\big(\gcd(a,b)\big)^{2}}\,. |  |

Applying this transformation on any two neighbor elements of a given sequence of integer numbers, we obtain a new Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) -generated sequence. We write it on the next row, such that each term is placed in the middle, below its parents, like in a contemporary presentation of the Pascal arithmetic triangle. One difference, however, lies in the fact that in our cases of interest the first row is long.

A construction similar to that in Figure 1, but with Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) replaced by the absolute difference function leads to a table whose appearance seems simpler (see Guy [16, Problem A10]). In that case the numbers quickly get smaller and one notices that the numbers on the left edge of the triangle are equal to one. Proth [24] thought that he proved that fact, but his argument was incorrect and the problem remained open. Later Gilbreath rediscovered the property and the statement is known as Glibreath’s Conjecture. Odlyzko [22] verified the conjecture for triangles of size < π ⁡ ( 10 13) <\pi(10^{13}). The apparent simplicity of the rows of higher generations hides deep problems involving gaps of different orders between primes, on which current results only scrape the surface of a field that remains untouchable by known techniques. Although, Odlyzko [22] observed that there are chances to overcome the involved difficulties. Reasoning heuristically, he concluded that the taming property of the numbers on the left edge of such a construction should be generally valid. Indeed, he showed that due to iterativity, this should be a characteristic property of lots of other sequences, provided their gaps to neighbor elements are random and relatively small.

On the contrary, in our case, one sees that the numbers become large, growing in augmented waves. Notice that by definition it follows that they are all square free. Looking for an analogue of Gilbreath’s conjecture, we consider the prime factorization of the first element of each row of the construction whose first part is presented in Figure 1. Denote by d m d_{m} the first element of row m m, for m ≥ 0 m\geq 0. The factorization into primes of the first few of them is given in Table 1. Analyzing this table, we remark a striking phenomenon: the number of prime factors of each of these numbers is a power of 2 2. The following theorem that will be proved twice, in Sections 4 and 5, establishes this fact.

###### Theorem 1.

The number of prime factors of d m d_{m} is a power of 2 2, for any m ≥ 0 m\geq 0.

Here the sequence of exponents is itself remarkable. For any m ≥ 0 m\geq 0, let

 | δ m:= log ⁡ ( the number of prime factors of d m) log ⁡ 2. \delta_{m}:=\frac{\log\big(\text{the number of prime factors of $d_{m}$}\big)}{\log 2}\,. |  |

Kimberling [17]). Indeed, the subsequence of terms having even rank coincides with the original sequence. And this fact occurs for sub-subsequences, also, again and again, ad infinitum. Precisely this follows by

 | δ t ​ 2 s = δ t, for any 0 ≤ t and 1 ≤ s. \delta_{t2^{s}}=\delta_{t},\quad\text{for any $0\leq t$ and $1\leq s$.} |  | (1) |

We do not know a closed formula for δ m \delta_{m}, but we know that it verifies many other recursive formulas. Thus, given an initial group of 2 s 2^{s} terms for any s ≥ 1 s\geq 1, we obtain the next group of 2 s 2^{s} terms just by incrementing by 1 1 all the known terms, that is,

 | δ 2 s + j = δ j + 1, for any 0 ≤ j ≤ 2 s − 1 and 1 ≤ s. \delta_{2^{s}+j}=\delta_{j}+1,\quad\text{for any $0\leq j\leq 2^{s}-1$ and $1\leq s$.} |  |

Next on the second row, we put: δ 16 = 1 \delta_{16}=1, δ 17 = 2 \delta_{17}=2, …, δ 31 = 5 \delta_{31}=5, and so on. There, nine distinct random colors are chosen for each of the possible values of δ m \delta_{m}. We remark that this square is symmetric with respect to the north-west–south-east diagonal. Another ’complementary symmetry’ is featured by the table with respect to the southwest–north-east diagonal. We observe that all the elements on this diagonal have the same color, that of δ 15 = 4 \delta_{15}=4. We also see that, if δ r \delta_{r} and δ s \delta_{s} are on squares symmetric with respect to the south-west–north-east diagonal, then δ r + δ s = 8 \delta_{r}+\delta_{s}=8.

Furthermore, the square from Figure 2 may also be generated starting with the basic ’number motif’ (or ’colored motif’) of the 4 × 4 4\times 4 square situated on the north-west corner. This motif is repeated with a period of length 4 4 again and again along the horizontal or vertical lines, and also along the 45 ∘ 45^{\circ} diagonals. Along the way, the only change is the simultaneous increment or decrement of all the elements in the number motifs by a specific value. The values that change the number motifs evolve 2 2 -adically in a remarkable way. To better emphasize these properties, in Figure 3 we show the analogue squares generated in a similar way, by writing the sequence { δ m } m ≥ 0 \{\delta_{m}\}_{m\geq 0} in rows of 32 32 and 64 64 at a time, respectively. Notice also that one may consider as the basic number motif a 2 × 2 2\times 2 square and also any larger square of size 2 t × 2 t 2^{t}\times 2^{t}. These symmetries occur also in analogue packages of the sequence { δ m } m ≥ 0 \{\delta_{m}\}_{m\geq 0} in boxes embedded in higher dimensional spaces. Despite of this and the simplicity of their construction, the pictures are on the verge between regularity and randomness.

Sequence { δ m } m ≥ 0 \{\delta_{m}\}_{m\geq 0} has many other interesting properties. It is sequence A000120 from Sloane’s On-line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences and it is related to other remarkable sequences, cf. [26] and the references therein. They are mainly associated with binary representations, because δ m \delta_{m} equals the number of 1 1 ’s from the m m -th row of Pascal triangle modulo 2 2. Further, this links them to Fermat numbers, constructible polygons and Bernoulli numbers. A short survey on these ties is presented in [13, Section 4].

Further inspection of the factorization of entries in Figure 1 reveals that ω ⁡ ( a) \omega(a), the number of prime factors of a a, is the same for all integers a a situated on the same horizontal line. This is the object of our next theorem that will be proved in Section 5.

###### Theorem 2.

We have ω ⁡ ( d m) = ω ⁡ ( a m, n) \omega(d_{m})=\omega(a_{m,n}), for all m ≥ 0 m\geq 0, n ≥ 1 n\geq 1.

Deeper properties of the evolution function Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) may be investigated in other contexts, where Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) is applied to integers that are not necessarily square free as in the present case. Various interesting questions arise for instance when the role of the starting sequence (played by the primes in this article) is taken by the sequence of natural numbers, or by others, such as the Fibonacci sequence. If the first row is a line of binomial coefficients ( n 0) \binom{n}{0}, ( n 1), …, ( n n) \binom{n}{1},\dots,\binom{n}{n}, from Pascal’s arithmetic triangle, the generated table is a bounded triangle. Looking at their p p -renderings, one sees striking differences in these pictures when one of p p or n n is kept fixed and the other varies, even slightly (cf. [13]).

Working only with integers that are products of distinct primes, enables one to develop techniques that may be useful in the general case. In the following sections we elaborate such a method that may be useful in all cases and the main results stated above will follow, too.

## 2. A related game

In this section we apparently depart from the original problem, experimenting with a setting that is more general and more particular at the same time. For this, we lift the left edge barrier. This extends the field of the game to also allow in the new generations entries from the left side. Next, we assume here that the entries are elements of 𝔽 2 = ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ \mathbb{F}_{2}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} and the operation Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) is replaced by the difference in 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2}. This is close to the essence of the end phase of an n n -absolute difference game cf. the authors’ [12]. In the last century, this automaton was discovered independently, several times, by different authors. ## 4. The absolute difference game of exponents – proof of Theorem 1

The Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) operation is in fact an absolute difference function on the exponents. Working with arbitrary numbers, this is best seen by taking their localized p p -scans. Thus, we have

 | Z ⁡ ( p s, p t) = p ( s + t) − 2 ​ min ⁡ ( s, t) = p | s − t |. Z(p^{s},p^{t})=p^{(s+t)-2\min(s,t)}=p^{|s-t|}. |  |

## 5. The game revisited – proofs of Theorems 1 and 2

In previous sections we saw that one gets the best view of the field of the game on exponents by considering it as a superposition of p p -renderings for all prime numbers p p. In Figure 7, there is an example of such a rendering for p = 101 p=101, the 26 26 -th prime. In Section 3 we have noticed that incomplete Sierpinski triangles repeat in a cycle of length 32 32 (the first number that is a power of 2 2 and is larger than 26 26). This is just an instance of an arbitrary prime and there is a rewarding way to look on it. Indeed, let T ∞ T_{\infty} be an infinite Sierpinski triangle with one vertex on the top and having cells corresponding to the honeycomb network of our game. We assume that T ∞ T_{\infty} runs across the honeycomb moving from left to right, one step at a time. Furthermore, we assume that triangle T ∞ T_{\infty} changes to a new color its nonempty cells at every step.

For n ≥ 1 n\geq 1, we denote by 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} the image of T ∞ T_{\infty} that lies over the honeycomb of the game when its vertex from the top lies over the n n -th cell of the topmost line. Thus, 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} is the p p -rendering of the game corresponding to p = p n p=p_{n}, the n n -th prime. For example, in Figures 6 and 7 are shown the upper parts of 𝒫 1 \mathcal{P}_{1}, 𝒫 2 \mathcal{P}_{2}, …, 𝒫 12 \mathcal{P}_{12} and 𝒫 26 \mathcal{P}_{26}, respectively. With these realizations, our number game from Figure 1 corresponds to the mosaic of colors developed by the superposition of all 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n}, n ≥ 1 n\geq 1.

Now let us look at an arbitrary cell m ≥ 0 m\geq 0, n ≥ 1 n\geq 1. The geometrical constrains show that the prime numbers p k p_{k}, k ≥ 1 k\geq 1, for which a nonempty cell of 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}_{k} lies over the cell of a m, n a_{m,n} must satisfy the inequality p n ≤ p k ≤ p m + n p_{n}\leq p_{k}\leq p_{m+n}. Moreover, we need to consider only those primes p k p_{k} for which a nonempty cell of 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}_{k} lies over the cell ( m, n) (m,n). Let 𝒮 m \mathcal{S}_{m} be the set of nonzero numbers from the m m -th row of Sierpinski triangle, that is,

 | 𝒮 m:= { r: ( m r) ≡ 1 ( mod 2), 0 ≤ r ≤ m }. \mathcal{S}_{m}:=\Big\{r\colon\ \binom{m}{r}\equiv 1\pmod{2},\ \ 0\leq r\leq m\Big\}\,. |  |

Then, we have obtained a closed form expression for any entry in Figure 1. This is

 | a m, n = ∏ r ∈ 𝒮 m p n + r. a_{m,n}=\prod_{r\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}p_{n+r}\,. |  |

In particular, we have proved both Theorem 1 and Theorem 2.

## 6. Conclusion and Future Directions

We discussed the outcome of the iterated application of a simple arithmetical function Z ⁡ ( a, b) Z(a,b), which operates like a recorder of the result produced by the plastic collision of two elementary particles. Thus Z ⁡ ( a, b) Z(a,b) is a product of powers of primes p d p p^{d_{p}} and locally, for any prime p p, the exponent d p d_{p} is the absolute difference of the exponents of p p in a a and b b. This paper leads to some further directions of research. Some open problems that naturally arise are presented below.

Sequence d m d_{m} from Table 1 increases in dyadically enhanced twists. It combines the size of primes with the selection process structured by the Sierpinski triangle sieve. Therefore, the following natural questions arise.

Question 1. Find accurate lower and upper bounds for

 | min x ≤ m ≤ y ⁡ d m, min x ≤ m ≤ y ⁡ ω ⁡ ( d m), max x ≤ m ≤ y ⁡ d m and max x ≤ m ≤ y ⁡ ω ⁡ ( d m). \min_{x\leq m\leq y}d_{m},\ \ \min_{x\leq m\leq y}\omega(d_{m}),\ \ \max_{x\leq m\leq y}d_{m}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \max_{x\leq m\leq y}\omega(d_{m}). |  |

Question 2. Estimate the following averages:

 | ∑ x ≤ m ≤ y ω ⁡ ( d m) and ∑ x ≤ m ≤ y d m. \sum_{x\leq m\leq y}\omega(d_{m})\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \sum_{x\leq m\leq y}d_{m}. |  |

Question 3. Starting with the sequence of positive integers instead of the primes on the first line and applying the same generating Z ⁡ ( ⋅, ⋅) Z(\cdot,\cdot) - rule, we obtain a triangle that is similar to the triangle in Figure 1 (see [13, Section 9]).
