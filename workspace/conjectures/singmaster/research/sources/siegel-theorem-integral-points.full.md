In [mathematics][1], **Siegel's theorem on integral points**states that a curve of [genus][2] greater than zero has only finitely many integral points over any given [number field][3].

The theorem was first proved in 1929 by [Carl Ludwig Siegel][4] and was the first major result on [Diophantine equations][5] that depended only on the genus and not any special algebraic form of the equations. For *g*> 1 it was superseded by [Faltings' theorem][6] in 1983.

## Statement

[[edit][7]]

**Siegel's theorem on integral points:**For a [smooth][8] [algebraic curve][9]*C*of [genus][2]*g*defined over a [number field][3]*K*, presented in [affine space][10] in a given coordinate system, there are only finitely many points on *C*with coordinates in the [ring of integers][11]*O*of *K*, provided *g*> 0.

## History

[[edit][12]]

In 1926, Siegel proved the theorem effectively in the special case g = 1 {\displaystyle g=1}[image: {\displaystyle g=1}], so that he proved this theorem conditionally, provided the [Mordell's conjecture][13] is true.

In 1929, Siegel proved the theorem unconditionally by combining a version of the [Thue–Siegel–Roth theorem][14], from [diophantine approximation][15], with the [Mordell–Weil theorem][16] from [diophantine geometry][17] (required in Weil's version, to apply to the [Jacobian variety][18] of *C*).

In 2002, [Umberto Zannier][19] and [Pietro Corvaja][20] gave a new proof by using a new method based on the [subspace theorem][21]. [1]

## Effective versions

[[edit][22]]

Siegel's result was ineffective for g ≥ 2 {\displaystyle g\geq 2}[image: {\displaystyle g\geq 2}] (see [effective results in number theory][23]), since [Thue][24] 's method in diophantine approximation also is ineffective in describing possible very good rational approximations to almost all [algebraic numbers][25] of degree d ≥ 5 {\displaystyle d\geq 5}[image: {\displaystyle d\geq 5}]. Siegel proved it effectively only in the special case g = 1 {\displaystyle g=1}[image: {\displaystyle g=1}] in 1926. Effective results in some cases derive from [Baker's method][26].
