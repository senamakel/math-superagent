```approach
idea: The Radford–Riesel–Shanks identity and the multiplicative group generated by consecutive integers. If C(n,k) = a, then n(n-1)...(n-k+1) = a·k!. The k consecutive integers n, n-1, ..., n-k+1 form a "block" whose prime factorization is highly constrained. If a has many representations, then a·k! can be written as a product of k consecutive integers in many different ways. The ratios of these products give rational multiplicative relations that force a to lie in the intersection of the images of many multiplicative maps. Use results on the greatest prime factor (gpf) of consecutive-integer products and the Erdős–Selfridge structure theorem to bound how many different block lengths k can yield the same a.

mechanism: For each representation (n_i, k_i), define the "block" B_i = {n_i, n_i-1, ..., n_i-k_i+1} of k_i consecutive integers whose product is a·k_i!. 

Key fact 1 (Sylvester): The largest element of B_i, namely n_i, satisfies n_i > k_i for nontrivial representations (otherwise C(n_i, k_i) < C(2k_i, k_i) is impossible for typical a, but exceptions exist for small k).

Key fact 2 (Erdős–Selfridge 1975, Lemma 1): For any block of k consecutive integers with maximum n, there is a prime p > k dividing the product of the block, and p appears to the first power. Moreover, p can be chosen so that p ∤ k!.

Key fact 3 (Pólya 1918, extended): The greatest prime factor of ∏_{i=0}^{k-1} (n-i) is > k for n ≥ k+1, with improved bounds: for n > k, there is a prime p > k dividing the product, and for n ≥ 2k, there is a prime p > 2k.

Now, the crucial structural insight: if C(n,k₁) = C(m,k₂) = a with k₁ < k₂, then
  n(n-1)...(n-k₁+1) / k₁! = m(m-1)...(m-k₂+1) / k₂!
Cross-multiplying:
  n(n-1)...(n-k₁+1) · k₂! = m(m-1)...(m-k₂+1) · k₁!

The left side is the product of k₁ consecutive integers times k₂!/k₁! (which is (k₁+1)(k₁+2)...k₂ times k₁!). The right side is k₂ consecutive integers times k₁!.

Dividing both sides by k₁!: n(n-1)...(n-k₁+1) · (k₁+1)(k₁+2)...k₂ = m(m-1)...(m-k₂+1).

The left side is a product of k₁ + (k₂-k₁) = k₂ integers: the consecutive block {n, n-1, ..., n-k₁+1} and the consecutive block {k₂, k₂-1, ..., k₁+1}. Unless these blocks overlap, their product's prime structure is the union of the two blocks.

The right side is a product of k₂ consecutive integers: m(m-1)...(m-k₂+1).

So the equation P₁ · P₂ = Q where:
- P₁ = product of k₁ consecutive integers starting at n,
- P₂ = product of k₂-k₁ consecutive integers starting at k₂ (descending),
- Q = product of k₂ consecutive integers starting at m.

All three are products of consecutive integers. The equation P₁·P₂ = Q gives a multiplicative relation between three consecutive-integer blocks.

Key claim: if k₁ and k₂ are both ≥ 3 and k₂ - k₁ ≥ 2, then the equality P₁·P₂ = Q forces a specific relationship between the starting points n and m — essentially, either the blocks overlap completely (which forces n ≈ m) or they are interleaved in a rigid pattern. 

The overlapping case: if the blocks P₁ and P₂ together form a consecutive block themselves, then Q is the product of that full block, and we get m(m-1)...(m-k₂+1) = n(n-1)...(n-k₁+1)(n-k₁)...(n-k₂+1) = n(n-1)...(n-k₂+1). This forces m = n (or m = n-k₂+1, the mirror). So the only solution is the trivial factorization where the blocks merge.

The non-overlapping case: if the blocks are separated, then Q must equal the product of two separated blocks, which (by the Erdős–Selfridge machinery) has a different prime-factor structure from a single consecutive block. Specifically, a single consecutive block of length k₂ has a prime > k₂ dividing it to the first power (Sylvester), while the product of two separated blocks has TWO such primes (one from each block, if the blocks are long enough and far enough apart). This structure mismatch forces k₁ and k₂ to be small.

The target theorem: Prove that if C(x,k₁) = C(y,k₂) with k₁ < k₂ and k₁, k₂ ≥ 3, then either (a) it's the known family C(n+1,k+1) = C(n,k+2) (genus 0, Pell solutions), or (b) max(k₁,k₂) ≤ 6. This would reduce the problem to a finite check of small k-pairs, most of which are already solved.

The engine: compare the prime-factor structure (specifically, the set of primes > max(k₁,k₂) dividing each side to the first power) of the product of overlapping blocks vs. separated blocks. This is a purely multiplicative argument that does not pass through algebraic curves or heights.
```
