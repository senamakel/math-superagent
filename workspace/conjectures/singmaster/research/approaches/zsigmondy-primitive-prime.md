```approach
idea: Zsigmondy–Bang primitive prime divisor theorem applied to the consecutive-integer blocks in C(x,k1)=C(y,k2) — each representation of a with k above a threshold carries a prime that divides the corresponding falling-factorial block for the "first time," giving each representation a unique prime signature. Coupling this with Sylvester's theorem (each block of length k has a prime > k) yields a bound on N(a) from the prime factorisation of a alone, without passing through the algebraic curve or heights.

mechanism: The equal-products reduction of C(x,k1)=C(y,k2) is
  x(x-1)...(x-k1+1)·k2! = y(y-1)...(y-k2+1)·k1!.
Cancel the factorial gcd, leaving an equality of two products of consecutive integers: P₁(x,k1)·c₂ = P₂(y,k2)·c₁, where c₁,c₂ are rational constants bounded by the factorials. For a fixed a = C(n,k), consider the set of all representations (n_i,k_i). Each block of k_i consecutive integers ending at n_i contributes its prime support to a. The key structural fact:

(1) **Sylvester (1892):** The product of k consecutive integers each > k is divisible by a prime p > k. So every representation with k_i ≥ 2 contributes a prime p_i > k_i to the factorisation of a. For distinct k_i, the lower bounds p_i > k_i differ; if k_i are all distinct, the primes p_i might overlap or may be forced to be distinct.

(2) **Primitive prime divisor (Zsigmondy 1892, Bang 1886):** For the sequence a^n − b^n there exists a prime dividing some term that does not divide any earlier term. **Caveat, corrected this cycle:** the earlier note's "Granville–Ramaré (1996, J. London Math. Soc. 54)" primitive-prime-divisor theorem for products of consecutive integers is a PHANTOM citation — their 1996 paper is "Explicit bounds on exponential sums and the scarcity of squarefree binomial coefficients" (Mathematika 43, 73–107), a different subject; no such theorem is on disk or in the literature under that name (see `research/notes/zsigmondy-phantom-citation.md`). The SYLVESTER step is real and grounded: Sylvester (1892); sharpened by **Laishram–Shorey (Acta Arith. 120 (2005) 299–306, held)**: P(Δ(n,k)) > 2k for n > max(k+13, (279/262)k), and P(Δ(n,k)) > 1.97k for n > k+13. Whether a genuine "primitive" prime (dividing the block but no smaller-start block of length k) exists for falling-factorial blocks is an OPEN question — that is the honest gap the phantom was covering (Zsigmondy itself applies to a^n − b^n, not to consecutive-integer products). The step below is therefore conditional on the existence of such a theorem, which is not established.

The coupling: if a has many representations (n_i,k_i), then each representation with n_i large relative to k_i contributes a primitive prime divisor that cannot appear in any other representation with a SMALLER n. Since a is fixed, its prime factorisation is fixed — the primitive prime from representation i must divide a, and the same prime cannot be primitive for two different representations with the same k. This forces a bound: either all representations have small n (bounded by some function of k), or the largest representation's primitive prime forces a contradiction with the number of available prime factors of a.

The key difference from the refuted Erdős–Selfridge approach: that approach relied on the OPEN "exponent-1 prime" conjecture. THIS approach would use a primitive prime divisor theorem for products of consecutive integers, which is NOT established (the Granville–Ramaré primitive-prime theorem was a phantom; see above). The Sylvester/Laishram–Shorey prime gives p > 2k (existence) but NO primitiveness; the engine that "each new representation with large n forces a new prime factor of a" is conditional on an unproved statement. As stated, this approach is NOT a proof — its standing is "conditional on a missing theorem".
```
