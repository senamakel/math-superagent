idea: Frey-curve / modular method (Darmon–Granville, Ribet level-lowering, Bennett–Skinner) applied to the equal-products-of-consecutive-integers equation — the Diophantine reduction of C(x,k1) = C(y,k2). This is the same machinery that proved Fermat's Last Theorem and Catalan's conjecture, applied here to classify all integer solutions when one of the column indices is fixed.

mechanism: The equality C(x,k1) = C(y,k2) is equivalent to the equal-products equation
  x(x-1)...(x-k1+1) · k2! = y(y-1)...(y-k2+1) · k1!.
For fixed k1, this is an equation of the form
  f(x) = g(y)   where f(x) = x(x-1)...(x-k1+1) · k2! / gcd(k1!,k2!)
and similarly for g(y). When k1 and k2 are small (the boundary regime where all witnesses live), the integers involved have controlled prime support, and the Frey curve attached to a hypothetical large solution has a conductor whose radical is bounded by the primes dividing k1!k2! times the primes ≤ max(k1,k2). Ribet's level-lowering then forces a congruence between a newform of controlled level and an elliptic curve, giving an effective contradiction beyond some computable bound.

status: refuted
killed-by: The modular method requires the Diophantine equation to be transformable into a generalized Fermat form A·x^p + B·y^q = C·z^r (three-term additive), to which a Frey curve E and a Galois representation ρ on E[p] can be attached. The equal-products equation is MULTIPLICATIVE: x(x-1)...(x-k1+1)·k2! = y(y-1)...(y-k2+1)·k1! — a product equality, not a sum. No known transformation converts this into a three-term additive equation for general k1,k2. Bennett's successful modular-method results (2001, 2013) handle equations of the form (x+1)...(x+k) = y^n where one side is a PURE POWER — the pure power y^n is essential for the Frey curve construction (it provides the p in E[p]). The binomial equal-products equation has BOTH sides as products of consecutive integers; neither side is a pure power unless one of k1,k2 is 1 (trivial column) or both sides collapse to a single factor. Even for the triangular column k1=2: x(x-1)·k! = 2·y(y-1)...(y-k+1). The RHS is a product of k consecutive integers, not a pure power, so no prime exponent p can be extracted uniformly. BMSST 2008 solved (2,5) using elliptic logarithms (Baker's method), NOT the modular method. The full text of BMSST (held, research/sources/bugeaud-hyperelliptic-2008.full.md) confirms this: their method is Baker/Matveev linear forms in elliptic logarithms, combined with Mordell-Weil sieving — zero modularity. No source applies the Darmon–Granville modular machinery to the equal-products equation; the search surfaces only Bennett's pure-power results and the general modular-method literature (Darmon–Granville 1995, Ribet 1990), none of which address equal consecutive-integer blocks. The approach describes a template that does not exist for this equation shape.
