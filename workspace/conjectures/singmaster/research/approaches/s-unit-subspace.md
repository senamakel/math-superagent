```approach
idea: S-unit equations via the Schmidt Subspace Theorem — bound N(a) for each
  fixed a by noting that all representations (n,k) with C(n,k)=a give S-integers
  n that are solutions of the unit equation n(n-1)...(n-k+1) = a·k! over a
  common set of primes S. The Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt bound on the number of
  solutions to S-unit equations depends only on |S| and the number of terms,
  not on the magnitudes — giving a bound on N(a) that is independent of a, up to
  the dependence of |S| on a.

mechanism: Fix a > 1. Every representation (n,k) with C(n,k) = a satisfies:
  n(n-1)...(n-k+1) = a · k!
  Let S be the set of prime divisors of a, plus all primes ≤ max_i k_i where the
  k_i are the column indices of all representations of a. Since k ≤ log_2(a)
  (as C(2k,k) ≥ 2^k), we have max k_i ≤ log_2(a), so |S| ≤ ω(a) + π(log_2 a).

  For each representation, write the equation as:
    n(n-1)...(n-k+1) − a·k! = 0
  This is NOT directly an S-unit equation. The S-unit approach works by
  considering the DIFFERENCES between representations: if C(n,k) = C(m,l) = a,
  then:
    n(n-1)...(n-k+1)·l! = m(m-1)...(m-l+1)·k!
  This is an equality of products of terms, each term being an S-integer (each
  factor n, n-1, ..., n-k+1 is a difference of S-integers, but not necessarily
  an S-unit).

  The proper framing: rewrite the equal-products equation as a unit equation.
  For each representation (n_i, k_i), define:
    P_i = n_i(n_i-1)...(n_i-k_i+1) = a·k_i!
  The ratio P_i / P_j = k_i! / k_j! = (some rational number).
  More usefully: for fixed a, all products n_i(n_i-1)...(n_i-k_i+1) / k_i! = a
  are equal. Consider the multiplicative group G generated by the primes in S.
  Each factor n_i - j (0 ≤ j < k_i) is a positive integer whose prime factors
  all lie in S (since the product equals a·k_i!, and k_i! only involves primes
  ≤ k_i, and a only involves primes dividing a). So each n_i is an S-integer
  (specifically, n_i itself may have prime factors outside S — this is the gap).

  Correction: the n_i themselves are NOT constrained to have prime support in S.
  Only their falling factorial product is. So the S-unit framing doesn't directly
  apply to the n_i. The correct S-unit framing is on the DIFFERENCES:
    C(n,k) = C(m,l) ⇒ n(n-1)...(n-k+1)·l! = m(m-1)...(m-l+1)·k!
  After canceling common factors, we get an equation of the form:
    A·∏_{i∈I} (n-i) = B·∏_{j∈J} (m-j)
  where A,B are composed of primes in S. Each side is a product of S-integers.
  This is an equation of the form:
    ∏_{r=1}^{R} u_r = constant
  where each u_r is an integer with prime support in S ∪ {primes dividing the
  (n-i) or (m-j)}. 

  The key structural fact that makes this work: for a GIVEN a, the n_i across
  all representations are bounded in terms of the largest k_i. Specifically, for
  each k, C(n,k) = a determines n up to at most k values (polynomial of degree
  k), and n ≈ (a·k!)^{1/k} for large n. In particular, n_i ≤ a (since C(a,1)=a
  is always a solution, and C(n,k) > C(n,1) = n for k ≥ 2 when n > 2k). So all
  n_i ≤ a. This means the primes involved in any n_i are at most a, but that's
  not restrictive enough.

  The actual S-unit reduction that has been applied in the literature
  (Saradha-Shorey-Tijdeman, BST) reduces the equal-products equation to an
  S-unit equation in the shifted variables by using the structure of arithmetic
  progressions. For arithmetic progressions with equal products, SST 1995
  expresses each block as a shifted near-power and applies Baker's method.
  The subspace theorem route is mentioned in Tijdeman's survey (§8) for the
  equation x(x+d1)...(x+(L-1)d1) = y(y+d2)...(y+(M-1)d2) — Evertse (1984)
  used the subspace theorem to bound the number of solutions, but the bound
  is ineffective in the parameters (the subspace theorem gives finiteness but
  not computability).

  The specific new angle: rather than applying the subspace theorem to the
  equal-products equation per pair (which gives ineffective finiteness), apply
  it to the SYSTEM of equations constraining ALL representations of a
  simultaneously. The idea is that the set of all (n_i, k_i) with C(n_i,k_i)=a
  satisfies a family of S-unit equations where S = {primes dividing a} ∪
  {primes ≤ log_2 a}. By the subspace theorem, the number of solutions to this
  system is bounded by a function of |S|, and since |S| depends on a (through
  ω(a) and log_2 a), the bound N(a) ≤ f(ω(a), log_2 a) follows. To get a
  uniform constant, one would need to bound ω(a) in terms of something uniform
  — which is the remaining gap.

  Check whether the subspace theorem bound (e.g., Evertse 1984, Schlickewei 1990)
  gives a bound independent of a when ω(a) is bounded.
  Specifically: for a fixed number r = N(a) of representations, the hypothesis
  that C(n_i,k_i) = a for i=1..r gives r(r-1)/2 equalities of the form
  n_i(n_i-1)...(n_i-k_i+1)·k_j! = n_j(n_j-1)...(n_j-k_j+1)·k_i!.
  For each such equality, the prime factors of both sides belong to S. The
  subspace theorem (Evertse 1984, for S-unit equations in two variables: the
  equation x+y=1 has finitely many solutions in S-integers) bounds r.
  The concrete test: can the subspace theorem give r ≤ 8 as an absolute bound?
```
