In [mathematics][1] and the field of [number theory][2], the **Landau–Ramanujan constant**is the positive real number *b*that occurs in a theorem proved by [Edmund Landau][3] in 1908, [1] stating that for large x {\displaystyle x}[image: {\displaystyle x}], the number of [positive integers][4] below x {\displaystyle x}[image: {\displaystyle x}] that are the sum of two [square numbers][5] [behaves asymptotically as][6]

b x log ⁡ ( x). {\displaystyle {\dfrac {bx}{\sqrt {\log(x)}}}.}[image: {\displaystyle {\dfrac {bx}{\sqrt {\log(x)}}}.}]

This [constant][7]*b*was rediscovered in 1913 by [Srinivasa Ramanujan][8], in the first letter he wrote to [G.H. Hardy][9]. [2]

## Sums of two squares

[[edit][10]]

By the [sum of two squares theorem][11], the numbers that can be expressed as a sum of two squares of integers are the ones for which each [prime number][12] congruent to 3 mod 4 appears with an even exponent in their [prime factorization][13]. For instance, *45 = 9 + 36*is a sum of two squares; in its prime factorization, 3 2 × 5, the prime 3 appears with an even exponent, and the prime 5 is congruent to 1 mod 4, so its exponent can be odd.

Landau's theorem states that if N ( x) {\displaystyle N(x)}[image: {\displaystyle N(x)}] is the number of positive integers less than x {\displaystyle x}[image: {\displaystyle x}] that are the sum of two squares, then

lim x → ∞ ( N ( x) x log ⁡ ( x)) = b ≈ 0.764223653589220662990698731250092328116790541 {\displaystyle \lim _{x\rightarrow \infty }\ \left({\dfrac {N(x)}{\dfrac {x}{\sqrt {\log(x)}}}}\right)=b\approx 0.764223653589220662990698731250092328116790541}[image: {\displaystyle \lim _{x\rightarrow \infty }\ \left({\dfrac {N(x)}{\dfrac {x}{\sqrt {\log(x)}}}}\right)=b\approx 0.764223653589220662990698731250092328116790541}] (sequence [A064533][14] in the [OEIS][15]),

where b {\displaystyle b}[image: {\displaystyle b}] is the Landau–Ramanujan constant.

The Landau-Ramanujan constant can also be written as an infinite product:

b = 1 2 ∏ p ≡ 3 ( mod 4) ( 1 − 1 p 2) − 1 / 2 = π 4 ∏ p ≡ 1 ( mod 4) ( 1 − 1 p 2) 1 / 2. {\displaystyle b={\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}}\prod _{p\equiv 3{\pmod {4}}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p^{2}}}\right)^{-1/2}={\frac {\pi }{4}}\prod _{p\equiv 1{\pmod {4}}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}.}[image: {\displaystyle b={\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}}\prod _{p\equiv 3{\pmod {4}}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p^{2}}}\right)^{-1/2}={\frac {\pi }{4}}\prod _{p\equiv 1{\pmod {4}}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}.}]

## History

[[edit][16]]

This constant was stated by Landau in the limit form above; Ramanujan instead approximated N ( x) {\displaystyle N(x)}[image: {\displaystyle N(x)}] as an integral, with the same constant of proportionality, and with a slowly growing error term. [3]
