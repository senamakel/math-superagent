In [mathematics][4], more specifically in [combinatorics on words][5], a **Sturmian word**(**Sturmian sequence**or **billiard sequence**[1]) is a certain kind of infinitely long [sequence of characters][6]. The concept is named after [Jacques Charles François Sturm][7].

Such a sequence can be generated by considering a game of [English billiards][8] on a square table. The struck ball will successively hit the vertical and horizontal edges labelled 0 and 1 generating a sequence of letters. [2] This sequence is a Sturmian word.

## Definition

[[edit][9]]

Sturmian sequences can be defined strictly in terms of their combinatoric properties or geometrically as [cutting sequences][3] for lines of irrational slope or codings for [irrational rotations][10]. They are traditionally taken to be infinite sequences on the alphabet of the two symbols 0 and 1.

### Combinatorial definitions

[[edit][11]]

#### Sequences of low complexity

[[edit][12]]

For an infinite sequence of symbols *w*, let *σ*(*n*) be the [complexity function][13] of *w*; i.e., *σ*(*n*) = the number of distinct [contiguous subwords (factors)][14] in *w*of length *n*. Then *w*is Sturmian if *σ*(*n*) =*n*+ 1 for all*n*.

#### Balanced sequences

[[edit][15]]

A set *X*of binary strings is called *balanced*if the [Hamming weight][16] of elements of *X*takes at most two distinct values. That is, for any s ∈ X {\displaystyle s\in X}[image: {\displaystyle s\in X}] |*s*| 1 =*k*or |*s*| 1 =*k'*where |*s*| 1 is the number of 1s in *s*.

Let *w*be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s and let L n ( w) {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{n}(w)}[image: {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{n}(w)}] denote the set of all length-*n*subwords of *w*. The sequence *w*is Sturmian if L n ( w) {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{n}(w)}[image: {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{n}(w)}] is balanced for all *n*and *w*is not eventually periodic.

### Geometric definitions

[[edit][17]]

#### Cutting sequence of irrational

[[edit][18]]

Let *w*be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w*is Sturmian if for some x ∈ [0, 1) {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}] and some irrational θ ∈ ( 0, ∞) {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,\infty )}[image: {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,\infty )}], *w*is realized as the [cutting sequence][3] of the line f ( t) = θ t + x {\displaystyle f(t)=\theta t+x}[image: {\displaystyle f(t)=\theta t+x}].

#### Difference of Beatty sequences

[[edit][19]]

Let *w*= (*w**n*) be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w*is Sturmian if it is the difference of non-homogeneous [Beatty sequences][20], that is, for some x ∈ [0, 1) {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}] and some irrational θ ∈ ( 0, 1) {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,1)}]

w n = ⌊ n θ + x ⌋ − ⌊ ( n − 1) θ + x ⌋ {\displaystyle w_{n}=\lfloor n\theta +x\rfloor -\lfloor (n-1)\theta +x\rfloor }[image: {\displaystyle w_{n}=\lfloor n\theta +x\rfloor -\lfloor (n-1)\theta +x\rfloor }]

for all n {\displaystyle n}[image: {\displaystyle n}] or

w n = ⌈ n θ + x ⌉ − ⌈ ( n − 1) θ + x ⌉ {\displaystyle w_{n}=\lceil n\theta +x\rceil -\lceil (n-1)\theta +x\rceil }[image: {\displaystyle w_{n}=\lceil n\theta +x\rceil -\lceil (n-1)\theta +x\rceil }]

for all n {\displaystyle n}[image: {\displaystyle n}].

#### Coding of irrational rotation

[[edit][21]]

For θ ∈ [0, 1) {\displaystyle \theta \in [0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle \theta \in [0,1)}], define T θ: [0, 1) → [0, 1) {\displaystyle T_{\theta }:[0,1)\to [0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle T_{\theta }:[0,1)\to [0,1)}] by t ↦ t + θ mod 1 {\displaystyle t\mapsto t+\theta {\bmod {1}}}[image: {\displaystyle t\mapsto t+\theta {\bmod {1}}}]. x n = { 1 if T θ n ( x) ∈ [0, θ), 0 else. {\displaystyle x_{n}={\begin{cases}1&{\text{if }}T_{\theta }^{n}(x)\in [0,\theta ),\\0&{\text{else}}.\end{cases}}}[image: {\displaystyle x_{n}={\begin{cases}1&{\text{if }}T_{\theta }^{n}(x)\in [0,\theta ),\\0&{\text{else}}.\end{cases}}}]

Let *w*be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w*is Sturmian if for some x ∈ [0, 1) {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}[image: {\displaystyle x\in [0,1)}] and some irrational θ ∈ ( 0, ∞) {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,\infty )}[image: {\displaystyle \theta \in (0,\infty )}], *w*is the *θ*-coding of*x*.

## Discussion

[[edit][23]]

### Example

[[edit][24]]

A famous example of a (standard) Sturmian word is the [Fibonacci word][2]; [3] its slope is 1 / ϕ {\displaystyle 1/\phi }[image: {\displaystyle 1/\phi }], where ϕ {\displaystyle \phi }[image: {\displaystyle \phi }] is the [golden ratio][25].

### Balanced aperiodic sequences

[[edit][26]]

A set *S*of finite binary words is *balanced*if for each *n*the subset *S**n*of words of length *n*has the property that the [Hamming weight][16] of the words in *S**n*takes at most two distinct values. A **balanced sequence**is one for which the set of factors is balanced. A balanced sequence has at most *n*+1 distinct factors of length *n*. [4]: 43 An **aperiodic sequence**is one which does not consist of a finite sequence followed by a finite cycle. An aperiodic sequence has at least *n*+ 1 distinct factors of length *n*. [4]: 43 A sequence is Sturmian if and only if it is balanced and aperiodic. [4]: 43

Every word in the sequence 0}"}}'> 0}}"> ( s n) n > 0 {\displaystyle (s_{n})_{n>0}} 0}}"/> is a [prefix][35] of the next ones, so that the sequence itself [converges][36] to an infinite word, which is c α {\displaystyle c_{\alpha }}[image: {\displaystyle c_{\alpha }}].

The infinite sequence of words ( s n) n ≥ 0 {\displaystyle (s_{n})_{n\geq 0}}[image: {\displaystyle (s_{n})_{n\geq 0}}] defined by the above recursion is called the **standard sequence**for the standard word c α {\displaystyle c_{\alpha }}[image: {\displaystyle c_{\alpha }}], and the infinite sequence *d*= (*d*1, *d*2, *d*3, ...) of nonnegative integers, with *d*1 ≥ 0 and *d**n*> 0 (*n*≥ 2), is called its **directive sequence**.

A Sturmian word *w*over {0,1} is characteristic if and only if both 0*w*and 1*w*are Sturmian. [7]

### Frequencies

[[edit][37]]

If *s*is an infinite sequence word and *w*is a finite word, let μ*N*(*w*) denote the number of occurrences of *w*as a factor in the prefix of *s*of length *N*+ |*w*| − 1. If *μ**N*(*w*) has a limit as *N*→∞, we call this the **frequency**of *w*, denoted by *μ*(*w*). [4]: 73

For a Sturmian word *s*, every finite factor has a frequency. The [three-gap theorem][38] implies that the factors of fixed length *n*have at most three distinct frequencies, and if there are three values then one is the sum of the other two. [4]: 73

## Non-binary words

[[edit][39]]

For words over an alphabet of size *k*greater than 2, we define a Sturmian word to be one with complexity function *n*+*k*− 1. [6]: 6 They can be described in terms of cutting sequences for *k*-dimensional space. [6]: 84 An alternative definition is as words of minimal complexity subject to not being ultimately periodic. [6]: 85

## Associated real numbers

[[edit][40]]

A real number for which the digits with respect to some fixed base form a Sturmian word is a [transcendental number][41]. [6]: 64, 85

## Sturmian endomorphisms

[[edit][42]]

An endomorphism of the [free monoid][43]*B*∗ on a 2-letter alphabet *B*is **Sturmian**if it maps every Sturmian word to a Sturmian word [8] [9] and **locally Sturmian**if it maps some Sturmian word to a Sturmian word. [10] The Sturmian endomorphisms form a submonoid of the monoid of endomorphisms of *B*∗. [8]

Define endomorphisms φ and ψ of *B*∗, where *B*= {0,1}, by φ(0) = 01, φ(1) = 0 and ψ(0) = 10, ψ(1) = 0. Then *I*, φ and ψ are Sturmian, [11] and the Sturmian endomorphisms of *B*∗ are precisely those endomorphisms in the submonoid of the endomorphism monoid generated by {*I*,φ,ψ}. [9] [10] [7]

A morphism is Sturmian if and only if the image of the word 10010010100101 is a [balanced sequence][44]; that is, for each *n*, the [Hamming weights][16] of the subwords of length *n*take at most two distinct values. [9] [12]

## History

[[edit][45]]

Although the study of Sturmian words dates back to [Johann III Bernoulli][46] (1772), [13] [5]: 295 it was [Gustav A. Hedlund][47] and [Marston Morse][48] in 1940 who coined the term *Sturmian*to refer to such sequences, [5]: 295 [14] in honor of the mathematician [Jacques Charles François Sturm][7] due to the relation with the [Sturm comparison theorem][49]. [6]: 114
