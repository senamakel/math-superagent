The **faro shuffle**(American), **weave shuffle**(British), or **dovetail shuffle**is a method of [shuffling][6] [playing cards][7], in which half of the deck is held in each hand with the thumbs inward, then cards are released by the thumbs so that they fall to the table interleaved. Diaconis, Graham, and Kantor also call this **the technique**, when used in magic. [1]

[8] Comparison of a perfect faro out-shuffle and in-shuffle, the numbers denoting each card's positions before the shuffle

Mathematicians use the term "faro shuffle" to describe a precise rearrangement of a deck into two equal piles of 26 cards which are then interleaved perfectly. [2]

## Description

[[edit][9]]

A right-handed practitioner holds the cards from above in the left hand and from below in the right hand. The deck is separated into two preferably equal parts by simply lifting up half the cards with the right thumb slightly and pushing the left hand's packet forward away from the right hand. The two packets are often crossed and tapped against each other to align them. They are then pushed together on the short sides and bent either up or down. The cards will then alternately fall onto each other, ideally alternating one by one from each half, much like a [zipper][10]. A flourish can be added by springing the packets together by applying pressure and bending them from above. [3]

A game of [Faro][11] ends with the cards in two equal piles that the dealer must combine to deal them for the next game. According to the magician [John Maskelyne][12], the above method was used, and he calls it the "faro dealer's shuffle". [4] Maskelyne was the first to give clear instructions, but the shuffle was used and associated with faro earlier, as discovered mostly by the mathematician and magician [Persi Diaconis][13]. [5]

## Perfect shuffles

[[edit][14]]

The faro shuffle is a controlled shuffle that does not fully randomize a deck.

A perfect faro shuffle, where the cards are perfectly alternated, requires the shuffler to cut the deck into two equal stacks and apply just the right pressure when pushing the half decks into each other.

A faro shuffle that leaves the original top card at the top and the original bottom card at the bottom is known as an **out-shuffle**, while one that moves the original top card to second and the original bottom card to second from the bottom is known as an **in-shuffle**. These names were coined by the magician and computer programmer [Alex Elmsley][15]. [6]

An out-shuffle has the same result as removing the top and bottom cards, doing an in-shuffle on the remaining cards, and then replacing the top and bottom cards in their original positions. Repeated out-shuffles cannot reverse the order of the entire deck, only the middle n−2 cards. Mathematical theorems regarding faro shuffles tend to refer to out-shuffles.

An in-shuffle has the same result as adding one extraneous card at the top and one extraneous card at the bottom, doing an out-shuffle on the enlarged deck, and then removing the extraneous cards. Repeated in-shuffles can reverse the order of the deck.

If one can do perfect in-shuffles, then 26 shuffles will reverse the order of the deck and 26 more will restore it to its original order. [7]

In general, k {\displaystyle k}[image: {\displaystyle k}] perfect in-shuffles will restore the order of an n {\displaystyle n}[image: {\displaystyle n}] -card deck if 2 k ≡ 1 ( mod n + 1) {\displaystyle 2^{k}\equiv 1{\pmod {n+1}}}[image: {\displaystyle 2^{k}\equiv 1{\pmod {n+1}}}]. For example, 52 consecutive in-shuffles restore the order of a 52-card deck, because 2 52 ≡ 1 ( mod 53) {\displaystyle 2^{52}\equiv 1{\pmod {53}}}[image: {\displaystyle 2^{52}\equiv 1{\pmod {53}}}].

In general, k {\displaystyle k}[image: {\displaystyle k}] perfect out-shuffles will restore the order of an n {\displaystyle n}[image: {\displaystyle n}] -card deck if 2 k ≡ 1 ( mod n − 1) {\displaystyle 2^{k}\equiv 1{\pmod {n-1}}}[image: {\displaystyle 2^{k}\equiv 1{\pmod {n-1}}}]. For example, if one manages to perform eight out-shuffles in a row, then the deck of 52 cards will be restored to its original order, because 2 8 ≡ 1 ( mod 51) {\displaystyle 2^{8}\equiv 1{\pmod {51}}}[image: {\displaystyle 2^{8}\equiv 1{\pmod {51}}}]. However, only 6 faro out-shuffles are required to restore the order of a 64-card deck.

In other words, the number of in-shuffles required to return a deck of cards of even size *n*, to original order is given by the [multiplicative order][16] of 2 [modulo][17] (*n*+ 1).

For example, for a deck size of *n*=2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 ..., the number of in-shuffles needed are: 2, 4, 3, 6, 10, 12, 4, 8, 18, 6, 11, ... (sequence [A002326][18] in the [OEIS][19]).

According to [Artin's conjecture on primitive roots][20], it follows that there are infinitely many deck sizes which require the full set of *n*shuffles. [8]

The analogous operation to an out-shuffle for an infinite sequence is the [interleave sequence][21].

## As deck manipulation

[[edit][29]]

Magician [Alex Elmsley][15] discovered [*[citation needed][30]*] that a controlled series of in- and out-shuffles can be used to move the top card of the deck down into any desired position. The trick is to express the card's desired position as a [binary number][31], and then do an in-shuffle for each 1 and an out-shuffle for each 0.

For example, to move the top card down so that there are ten cards above it, express the number ten in binary (1010 2). Shuffle in, out, in, out. Deal ten cards off the top of the deck; the eleventh will be your original card. Notice that it doesn't matter whether you express the number ten as 1010 2 or 00001010 2; preliminary out-shuffles will not affect the outcome because out-shuffles always keep the top card on top.

In [mathematics][33], a perfect shuffle can be considered an element of the [symmetric group][34].

Analogously, the ( k, n) {\displaystyle (k,n)}[image: {\displaystyle (k,n)}] -perfect shuffle permutation [9] is the element of S k n {\displaystyle S_{kn}}[image: {\displaystyle S_{kn}}] that splits the set into *k*piles and interleaves them.
