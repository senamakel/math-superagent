Disadvantage in a game due to obligation to move

This article is about the disadvantageous requirement to move. For the move interposition tactic, see [Zwischenzug][1]. For other uses, see [Zugzwang (disambiguation)][2].

This article uses [algebraic notation][3] to describe chess moves.

**Zugzwang**( from [German][4] ' compulsion to move '; pronounced [[ˈtsuːktsvaŋ]][5]) is a situation found in [chess][6] and other [turn-based games][7] wherein one [player][8] is put at a disadvantage because of their obligation to make a move; a player is said to be "in zugzwang" when any legal move will worsen their position. [1]

Although the term is used less precisely in games such as chess, it is used specifically in [combinatorial game theory][9] to denote a move that directly changes the outcome of the game from a win to a loss. [2] [3] Putting the opponent in zugzwang is a common way to help the superior side win a game, and in some cases it is necessary in order to make the win possible. [4] More generally, the term can also be used to describe a situation where passing the turn, if this were allowed, would be the best move. [5] [6] [7]

The term *zugzwang*was used in German chess literature in 1858 or earlier, [8] and the first known use of the term in English was by [World Champion][10] [Emanuel Lasker][11] in 1905. [9] The concept of zugzwang was known to chess players many centuries before the term was coined, appearing in an [endgame study][12] published in 1604 by [Alessandro Salvio][13], one of the first writers on the game, and in [shatranj][14] studies dating back to the early 9th century, over 1000 years before the first known use of the term. International [chess notation][15] uses the symbol " ⊙ " to indicate a zugzwang position.

Positions with zugzwang occur fairly often in [chess endgames][16], especially in [king and pawn endgames][17] and elementary checkmates (such as a rook and king against a lone king). According to [John Nunn][18], positions of reciprocal zugzwang are surprisingly important in the analysis of endgames. [10] [11]

## Etymology

[[edit][19]]

The word comes from German *Zug*'move' + *Zwang*'compulsion', so that *Zugzwang*means 'being forced to make a move'. Originally the term was used interchangeably with the term *Zugpflicht*'obligation to make a move' as a general game rule. Games like chess and [checkers][20] have "zugzwang" (or "zugpflicht"): a player *must*always make a move on their turn even if this is to their disadvantage. Over time, the term became especially associated with chess.

According to chess [historian][21] [Edward Winter][22], the term had been in use in German chess circles in the 19th century. [8]

Pages 353–358 of the September 1858 *Deutsche Schachzeitung*had an unsigned article "Zugzwang, Zugwahl und Privilegien". [Friedrich Amelung][23] employed the terms Zugzwang, Tempozwang and Tempozugzwang on pages 257–259 of the September 1896 issue of the same magazine. When a perceived example of zugzwang occurred in the third game of the 1896–97 world championship match between Steinitz and Lasker, after 34...Rg8, the *Deutsche Schachzeitung*(December 1896, page 368) reported that "White has died of zugzwang".

The earliest known use of the term zugzwang in English was on page 166 of the February 1905 issue of *[Lasker's][11] Chess Magazine*. [9] The term did not become common in English-language chess sources until the 1930s, after the publication of the English translation of Nimzowitsch's *[My System][24]*in 1929. [8]
