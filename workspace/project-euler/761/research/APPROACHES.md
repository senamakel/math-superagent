## What closed, and why

Do not propose these again. A reason stated precisely is what makes that possible; one left blank makes this row worthless.

- [[angular-speed-safe-region]] (refuted): For a regular polygon the angular-speed-matching staging boundary {r ≤ ρ(φ)/v} IS the 1/v homothetic scaled copy of the pool — the very same safe region stewbasic's model uses. So constructing it from the angular-speed constraint is NOT an independent model; it reproduces the same region and, minimized maximally, the same V. It is a restatement of the ansatz, not a confirm-or-falsify. Moreover any genuinely DIFFERENT safe region (inner circle, diamond, octagon, square) is a strict LOWER BOUND on V — documented in the held math.SE n-gon thread (TMM/Jens: inner square 5.00, diamond 5.25, circle 5.27, octagon 5.38, vs the optimal homothetic scaled-pool 5.7886 for the square). The run's own independent game-encoding solver (code/indep_game_encoding.py) took exactly this "max over dash landing point from a staged region" form and produced 3.98 for the hexagon — below the true 5.055 — i.e. the same lower-bound trap. A safe-region route can land at or below the true value but cannot exceed it, so it can never falsify the stewbasic value upward; it can only ever confirm a (possibly strict) lower bound. The angular-MATCHING principle itself is real and sourced for the CIRCLE (Lady in the Lake auxiliary differential game: L stages at antipodal radius r=1/v where angular speeds match, then dashes — claim lady-in-the-lake-differential-game-equilibrium; also Hesterberg, Ponder This), but on polygons it degenerates into the escaper's lower-bound construction.
- [[schwarz-christoffel-conformal-mapping]] (refuted): The critical speed is NOT a conformal invariant. Disk and hexagon are conformally equivalent (an SC map exists — the premise is true), yet V_disk = 4.60333885 ≠ V_hexagon = 5.05505046. The run's OWN two oracle values for conformally-equivalent domains are a direct counterexample to the idea that the disk answer "pulls back" to the hexagon. Reason: the game's data are Euclidean distances (swimmer straight dash) and boundary ARC LENGTHS (runner time). Conformal maps preserve only angles, not either of these — they do not preserve the swimmer's straight-line distance (hexagon chords) or the runner's perimeter arc length. After any conformal map the game lives in a non-Euclidean Riemannian metric, and there is no Euclidean-interpretation relation between the two games. The Schwarz-Christoffel map exists but maps the Euclidean game to a curved-metrized game whose solution is a NEW problem, not the known flat-disk one. Searching the literature (web, research papers, and all 20 held primary sources) returns NO work applying conformal/Schwarz-Christoffel methods to escape or critical-speed pursuit games — this is an absence: the premise that the flat-disk answer transfers is false on its face (the two oracle values falsify it), independent of whether anyone has attempted it.

## Not yet taken to the literature

Nobody has checked whether these are known theory. Grounding one is cheaper than pursuing it: a named theorem arrives with its hypotheses, and a reformulation somebody already tried arrives with the reason it failed.

- [[eikonal-characteristics]]: For a fixed runner speed v, define the "earliest runner arrival time" at each boundary point Q as τ_R(Q) = (shortest perimeter distance from the runner's start to Q) / v. The swimmer, starting at the center, can reach a boundary point Q in time τ_S(Q) = |Q| (Euclidean distance, speed 1). Escape is possible at speed v iff there exists Q such that τ_S(Q) < τ_R(Q), i.e., min_Q [τ_R(Q) − τ_S(Q)] < 0. Now consider the value function Φ(x) = min_Q [τ_R(Q) + |x − Q|] — the minimum over boundary exit points of (runner arrival time + swim time from x to Q). This is the viscosity solution of the Eikonal equation |∇Φ| = 1 with boundary condition Φ(Q) = τ_R(Q). The swimmer can escape from x iff Φ(x) > 0 (if the earliest possible simultaneous arrival is after the runner gets there, the swimmer wins by going elsewhere; actually, the escape condition is more subtle — the swimmer wins if there exists Q with τ_R(Q) > |x − Q|, i.e., max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|] > 0). Define Ψ(x) = max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|]. Then Ψ satisfies |∇Ψ| = 1 (since it's a max of distance functions with offsets). The critical speed is the v where Ψ(0) = 0 when starting at the center. By the method of characteristics: each boundary point Q propagates information inward along the straight ray from Q toward the interior, carrying the value τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|. The value at an interior point is the maximum of these cones. The critical speed is where the center lies exactly on the intersection (shock) of two or more such cones — specifically, where the maximum switches between two different boundary exit points. For the hexagon with the runner starting at an edge midpoint, τ_R(Q) is a piecewise-linear function on the boundary (the perimeter distance divided by v). The function Ψ(0) = max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |Q|] is a 1D optimization over the hexagon boundary. Its zero as a function of v gives V. This formulation is structurally identical to the stewbasic boundary-time equalization but arrives there from PDE theory (the Eikonal equation / Hopf-Lax formula) rather than from geometric minimization — it is a completely independent derivation framework. The computational route: for the hexagon, parameterize the boundary by arc-length s, compute d_R(s) = min(s, 6−s)/v (runner distance from midpoint at s=0 to boundary point s), compute |Q(s)| = distance from center to boundary point at s, then V = max_s d_R(s)/|Q(s)| evaluated at V (fixed point). This is a 1D root-finding problem completely independent of the K/α machinery. The key difference from existing approaches: this formulates the entire problem as a single PDE/Eikonal equation rather than a pursuit-evasion game, and the critical speed emerges from the Hopf-Lax formula for the value function.
- [[polynomial-resultant-elimination]]: The hexagon (inradius 1, flat-top) has vertex coordinates and edge equations involving only √3, so every geometric quantity is algebraic of degree at most 2 over Q. Parameterize the exit point Q on one edge by a single rational parameter t ∈ [0,1] (or by coordinate x along the edge). The swimmer's staging point P is on the 1/v-scaled homothetic inner hexagon, centrally opposite the runner at R′ on the outer boundary. The chord distance |PQ|² is a quadratic polynomial in t with coefficients involving v and √3. The runner's perimeter distance from R′ to Q along the hexagon boundary is piecewise-linear in the edge index and linear in t. The critical-speed condition is: max_{edge e, t∈[0,1]} [perim(R′ → Q_e(t))] / |P − Q_e(t)| = v At the maximizing t (interior to the edge), the derivative of the ratio with respect to t vanishes, giving the first-order condition d/dt [perim/|PQ|] = 0. After clearing denominators, this is a polynomial equation in t and v² (and √3). Isolate √3 by squaring, then use resultants to eliminate t, yielding the minimal polynomial for v². For n=6 the result should be the known quadratic 9v⁴ − 240v² + 256 = 0, recovered by a route that never references K, α, arccos, or tan(xθ) = (x+n)tanθ. The key structural advantage: the hexagon's critical exit point lies on an edge adjacent to the edge opposite the runner's position (established pattern from the square case and the known K=2 result). So only a small number of edge candidates need to be checked, and the elimination can be done edge-by-edge with sympy's resultant or groebner.
- [[pontryagin-maximum-principle]]: The swimmer's dynamics are ẋ(t) = u(t) with ||u(t)|| ≤ 1, starting at x(0) = (0,0) (center). The runner's position s(t) on the boundary evolves as ṡ = ±v (choosing the direction that minimizes escape chance). The game ends at terminal time T when |x(T)| reaches the boundary at azimuth φ, with the runner at s(T). Escape succeeds if the swimmer's boundary arrival point is distinct from the runner's. The value is the minimum over runner strategies of the maximum over swimmer strategies of [escape success indicator]. Applying PMP: the Hamiltonian is H = p·u + q·(±v), where p ∈ ℝ² is the costate for the swimmer's position and q is the costate for the runner's boundary coordinate. The optimal swimmer control is u*(t) = p(t)/|p(t)| (bang-bang structure: the swimmer always moves at full speed in the direction of the costate). The costate evolves as ṗ = −∂H/∂x = 0 in the interior, so p is constant along straight-line dash segments. During the staging phase (if any), the swimmer moves to maintain a positional advantage; the costate is not constant because the Hamiltonian depends on position through the boundary-interaction term. The critical insight: the optimal trajectory consists of (1) a staging phase where the swimmer moves along a curve that keeps them "opposite" the runner in a sense defined by the costate dynamics, followed by (2) a terminal dash along a straight line (constant p). At the switching point between these phases, the costate p must be tangent to the staging curve (transversality). The critical speed v is the value where the maximum over all possible dash directions (parameterized by the terminal costate direction) of the escape margin equals zero. For the hexagon, the staging phase is constrained by the polygon geometry: the runner's motion is piecewise-constant-velocity along edges, creating piecewise dynamics for the adjoint. The critical condition reduces to: find v such that max_{dash direction, staging endpoint} [runner perimeter time − v × swimmer dash time] = 0, where the staging endpoint is constrained to lie on a curve determined by the adjoint equations. This is a different equation from the stewbasic max-ratio formula because the staging endpoint is not assumed to lie on a 1/v-scaled homothetic copy — it comes from the ODE.
