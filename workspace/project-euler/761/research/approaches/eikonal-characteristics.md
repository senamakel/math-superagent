idea: Eikonal equation / method of characteristics — the time-to-escape function T(x) for the swimmer satisfies the Eikonal equation |∇T| = 1 in the hexagon interior, with a boundary condition determined by the runner's perimeter travel time. The critical speed V is the value where the characteristic through the center just reaches the boundary before being overtaken by the boundary data. Solve this as a first-order PDE by the method of characteristics; the critical speed is determined by shock formation (intersection of characteristics from different boundary arcs).

mechanism: For a fixed runner speed v, define the "earliest runner arrival time" at each boundary point Q as τ_R(Q) = (shortest perimeter distance from the runner's start to Q) / v. The swimmer, starting at the center, can reach a boundary point Q in time τ_S(Q) = |Q| (Euclidean distance, speed 1). Escape is possible at speed v iff there exists Q such that τ_S(Q) < τ_R(Q), i.e., min_Q [τ_R(Q) − τ_S(Q)] < 0.

Now consider the value function Φ(x) = min_Q [τ_R(Q) + |x − Q|] — the minimum over boundary exit points of (runner arrival time + swim time from x to Q). This is the viscosity solution of the Eikonal equation |∇Φ| = 1 with boundary condition Φ(Q) = τ_R(Q). The swimmer can escape from x iff Φ(x) > 0 (if the earliest possible simultaneous arrival is after the runner gets there, the swimmer wins by going elsewhere; actually, the escape condition is more subtle — the swimmer wins if there exists Q with τ_R(Q) > |x − Q|, i.e., max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|] > 0).

Define Ψ(x) = max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|]. Then Ψ satisfies |∇Ψ| = 1 (since it's a max of distance functions with offsets). The critical speed is the v where Ψ(0) = 0 when starting at the center. By the method of characteristics: each boundary point Q propagates information inward along the straight ray from Q toward the interior, carrying the value τ_R(Q) − |x − Q|. The value at an interior point is the maximum of these cones. The critical speed is where the center lies exactly on the intersection (shock) of two or more such cones — specifically, where the maximum switches between two different boundary exit points.

For the hexagon with the runner starting at an edge midpoint, τ_R(Q) is a piecewise-linear function on the boundary (the perimeter distance divided by v). The function Ψ(0) = max_Q [τ_R(Q) − |Q|] is a 1D optimization over the hexagon boundary. Its zero as a function of v gives V. This formulation is structurally identical to the stewbasic boundary-time equalization but arrives there from PDE theory (the Eikonal equation / Hopf-Lax formula) rather than from geometric minimization — it is a completely independent derivation framework.

The computational route: for the hexagon, parameterize the boundary by arc-length s, compute d_R(s) = min(s, 6−s)/v (runner distance from midpoint at s=0 to boundary point s), compute |Q(s)| = distance from center to boundary point at s, then V = max_s d_R(s)/|Q(s)| evaluated at V (fixed point). This is a 1D root-finding problem completely independent of the K/α machinery. The key difference from existing approaches: this formulates the entire problem as a single PDE/Eikonal equation rather than a pursuit-evasion game, and the critical speed emerges from the Hopf-Lax formula for the value function.

The critical speed V satisfies V = max_{s∈[0,6)} f(s; V). This is a fixed-point equation in one variable. Solve by: for each candidate v, compute g(v) = max_s f(s; v); find v where g(v) = v. The maximization over s can be done analytically (derivative zero on each edge) or numerically. The circle self-validation: for a circle, d_R(s) = s (for s ∈ [0,π], then symmetric), |Q(s)| = 1, g(v) = π/v, fixed point v = π+1 — wait, that gives π+1, the red herring.
